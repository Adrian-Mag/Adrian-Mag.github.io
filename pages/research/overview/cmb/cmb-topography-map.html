CMB Topography Studies

An interactive timeline of core-mantle boundary topography papers, decoded through the ELEMENTS framework.

Scientific notes

A map of CMB topography studies

How hard is it to measure the shape of the core-mantle boundary? The literature has been arguing about this for forty years, and the argument has not converged. This interactive timeline places each study on a shared coordinate system: the ELEMENTS framework, which separates the model space, the data space, the model-data relation, the property map, the prior, the output type, and the calibration. When two papers disagree, the framework shows where the disagreement lives, not just that it exists.

How to read it. Time runs downward: the oldest paper is at the top, the newest at the bottom, with years marked on the axis at the left. The three columns are the method branches — body-wave mapping, normal modes, and the statistical line — and the tinted bands group the eras. Drag to pan down through the timeline; use the wheel or controls to zoom, and the middle button to re-fit. On a narrow screen the view opens on the body-wave column, so drag sideways to reach the normal-mode and statistical branches. Click a paper card for its ELEMENTS breakdown: model space, data, relation, property, prior, output, and calibration. Blue edges mean one paper builds on another; orange edges mean a critique; dashed yellow edges mean an analytical comparison added by the campaign, not a real citation. A green V badge means the paper has been through the full evidence-backed verification pass.

Not on the timeline: Russell (2022)

Russell (2022) sits upstream of the data space: it instantiates none of the seven ELEMENTS. The paper characterises ISC onset-pick measurement error — the noise model every body-wave study in this timeline assumes. It belongs here as a footnote, not as a node, because it has no model space, no relation, no property map, and no output to calibrate.

Off-timeline: Lau et al. (2017)

Lau et al. (2017) is the first node at which non-seismic information occupies the DATA-SPACE coordinate: the datum is the M2 body tide, GPS-measured vertical displacement at 456 stations (D = R^456). Non-seismic information had entered the corpus before, but always elsewhere — as a prior in trampert2004probabilistic, a tuning rule in sze2003, an external comparison in moulikEkstrom2016. Lau et al. is the first to put it in the data.

CMB topography is not inverted and not detectable from this data space. It enters only as a correction taken from nutation (excess ellipticity), and Extended Data Fig 2b tests five topography models including three corpus papers (li1991, morelli1987, sze2003). The aggregate matched-filter SNR is 0.03 to 0.10 — the corpus's CMB-amplitude dispute is invisible here. That is the point: a non-seismic datum can see the density structure but not the boundary shape.

The study occupies a unique cell in the framework: set-output / none-exercised. 34 models pass the rotation screen, and the Fig 4a histograms are the output. But the calibration is none exercised — the rotation null randomises the prediction over SO(3) while the datum is held fixed, so it is not a frequency under the observation kernel. It does not fit the three timeline lanes (body-wave, normal modes, statistical) because its data space is non-seismic, so it sits here as an off-timeline marker rather than a node.

The 20 km nobody believed

Creager & Jordan's 20 km is the number the later literature quotes as "the Creager-Jordan model", but the authors themselves judged it too large for dynamic support and preferred a chemical boundary layer interpretation. The 20 km is what you get when you assume the other two fields (mantle-side and core-side velocity) are identically zero, a prior choice dressed as a result.

Different problems, not different answers

The apparent "20 km vs 6 km" disagreement between Creager (1986) and Morelli (1987) is not a contradiction. Both papers correct their data for mantle velocity with a tomographic model and then invert for boundary structure. The difference is the data: with PKP alone, one fixed combination of topography, a mantle-side layer and a core-side layer produces no signal, so the three cannot be separated and Creager and Jordan's field is the combination the data can see, read as topography only by assuming the two layers are zero. Morelli added PcP, whose topography kernel has the opposite sign to PKP's, so topography and velocity produce distinguishably different data patterns. The 6 km is smaller because the signal that was actually velocity contamination is no longer attributed to topography. In ELEMENTS terms: different data spaces, different property separability — different problems, not different answers.

The 99.99% that wasn't

Morelli's headline quantitative claim is that PcP-only and PKP-only maps correlate at r = 0.701 (23 dof), rejecting a sub-CMB layer hypothesis at 99.99%. The verification study found that the 25 coefficient pairs include c₀⁰, the spherically symmetric mean core-radius term, which both inversions constrain well regardless of lateral structure. A shared c₀⁰ with zero lateral agreement already produces r ≈ 0.5–0.75. Remove degree 0 and the lateral correlation drops to r ≈ 0–0.4 (p ≈ 0.05–1.0). The qualitative agreement is real; the 99.99% level is not supported by the test as run.

The coupling axis begins

Doornbos & Hilton made the first move on the coupling axis: instead of assuming velocity is zero (Creager, Morelli), they enlarged the model space to carry topography and a laterally varying boundary-layer velocity jointly. The key finding is subtle: the smooth CMB (topographic variance ~1.5 km² vs Morelli's ~6 km²) comes from the data-space move (adding bottomside PKKP, which sees the boundary from below), not from the model-space enlargement itself. The joint inversion's role is to re-admit the velocity-contaminated phases without dragging topography up — a correct partition, not a smaller number.

PKKP: the phase that sees from below

PKKP reflects off the underside of the CMB — from inside the core. It is sensitive to topography but not to the mantle structure above the boundary, making it the cleanest topography channel in the body-wave family. The variance ladder tells the story: PcP 21.5 km², PKP 6.5-11.7 km², PKKP 1.3 km². The sixteenfold drop from PcP to PKKP is a data-space move, not a model-space move. Adding the velocity coordinate (model 5 → model 6) leaves the topographic variance unchanged at 1.5 km² — the coupling coordinate carries the partition, not the amplitude reduction.

A different datum: normal modes

Li, Giardini & Woodhouse (1991) brought a fundamentally independent data type to the CMB topography problem: free-oscillation spectral splitting, not body-wave travel times. Normal-mode splitting functions are even-degree, long-wavelength functionals of the whole Earth, sensing structure to degree 2l for a mode of degree l. The CMB topography is one component of a multi-field model space that also holds mantle heterogeneity and inner-core anisotropy. The modal CMB agrees with the even-degree body-wave map ‘to a fair extent’ but is smaller by a factor of order two — an independent confirmation of pattern, not amplitude.

The proportionality prior

Normal-mode splitting coefficients sense a single combination of bulk modulus, shear modulus and density (eq. 9), so P velocity, S velocity and density cannot be separated. LGW impose a hard proportionality prior (δln β = 2 δln α = 4 δln ρ, eq. 37) that collapses three fields to one scalar. The CMB topographic amplitude is conditional on this prior — change the ratio and the amplitude changes. Ishii & Tromp (1999) take the opposite route: they carry Vp, Vs, and density as separate fields with no proportionality and no flow relation, paying for it in under-determinacy and needing the gravity constraint. The within-normal-mode disagreement (LGW vs Ishii-Tromp) is thus a prior difference, not a physics difference.

Isostasy: the third closure

Obayashi & Fukao (1997) stated the velocity-topography ambiguity explicitly: the spherical average of their PcP anomaly is satisfiable by either a 3% velocity reduction in a 20 km basal layer or a 3 km CMB depression. To break it they assume isostatic compensation — a physical assumption that the CMB layer floats in equilibrium with the core — and the amplitude drops from ±7 km to ±1–2 km. This is the third distinct prior on the coupling axis (after Doornbos’s joint coordinate and LGW’s proportionality), and it confirms the thesis pattern: the topography number is set by which physical closure you assume, not by the data alone.

Cross-validation: the first predictive rule

Obayashi’s roughness parameter is selected by out-of-sample cross-validation — the only predictive prior-selection rule in the corpus. Every other paper tunes the prior against something already believed (a target resolution, an external geodetic value, a synthetic, or agreement with earlier maps). Cross-validation tunes it against data the inversion did not see, which is the only rule that could in principle be wrong in a way the paper would notice. The minima are shallow (∼0.1 s in a 2.1 s residual), and the three readings are tuned separately, so the maps are not directly comparable — but the rule itself is a genuine predictive check.

The imported amplitude

Ishii & Tromp (1999) is often cited as an independent normal-mode confirmation of ±5 km CMB topography. The verification study found that the amplitude is imported, not measured: p. 1235 states the data admit a range of amplitudes and the CMB ‘was required’ to have an overall amplitude of ±5 km to be compatible with Morelli & Dziewonski (1987). So the two are not independent — the ±5 km is a hard set prior, not an inference. The paper’s genuine contribution is the first whole-mantle density model (dense LLSVPs beneath Pacific and Africa), not a CMB topography measurement.

No flow relation

The reading note had recorded a ‘density → dynamic-topography flow relation (viscosity-dependent)’ in this paper. The verification study retracted it: there is no viscosity and no flow relation anywhere in the inversion. p. 1232 says the whole construction exists to circumvent needing one. Density and the three boundary topographies are independent parameters that enter eqs. (2) and (3) linearly and separately. The note had imported soldati2012’s architecture onto a paper that explicitly declines it. The LGW proportionality is also absent — Ishii & Tromp carry Vp, Vs, and density as separate fields, which is why their model is under-determined and needs the gravity constraint.

The statistical turn: amplitude without a map

Garcia & Souriau (2000) refused to invert for a map at all. Instead they estimate the statistical amplitude of CMB topography by stochastic analysis of PcP, PKP and PKKP residuals, reporting ±4 km at 95%. The verification study corrected the headline: the ±4 km is exactly 2σ of an assumed Gaussian for the topography field itself (p. 358: ‘If the CMB topography has a Gaussian distribution, it implies that 95% …’), not a computed quantile. The genuine inference is σCMB = 2.0 ± 0.7 km rms above 300 km, falling to ~1.0 km at 1000–1500 km where ‘the error bar is nearly of the same order as the topography amplitude’. The ±4 km is a property-map closure (carrying σ to a range under a distributional assumption), not a calibration. This is the corpus’s clearest instance of retreating to a property the data can support.

The checkerboard verdict

Garcia & Souriau ran a two-way checkerboard — topography-only input and D''-only input — with no damping. Both directions leak: ‘In each case, a strong signal is present in D'' and a weak signal is present in the topography.’ Their conclusion (p. 358): ‘it is hopeless to try to invert our data for mapping the CMB topography’ and ‘this result casts serious doubts on the validity of the CMB topographic models published up to now.’ The verification study notes the truncated SVD (100 of 440 singular values) is itself a prior acting on exactly the ill-conditioned directions where topography and D'' trade off, so the leakage is not fully independent of a regularisation choice — but it runs in both directions, which no single-direction smoothing artefact would produce. The stochastic estimate separates topography from D'' by an assumed mantle cancellation in eq. (4), which is exactly the separation the checkerboard proves these data cannot deliver.

The non-uniqueness node

Boschi & Dziewonski (2000) made the model space itself the object under test: four model classes (isotropic, anisotropic, +outer-core, both), each with free CMB topography. The PcP-vs-PKP CMB-map discrepancy is ‘evident and systematic’ and survives all four classes and the independent Engdahl 1998 data. CMB topography is a tradeoff nuisance, not a deliverable: a flat-CMB synthetic returns fictitious topography under ~1 km, ten times below the observed signal. The model-selection criterion (eq. 11) is morelli1987’s correlation-agreement device re-used in the negative direction (low r = 0.16 vs ~0.5 for PKPbc-vs-PKPdf), now filed as a diagnostic consistent-with, not diagnostic-of, inadequacy. The verification study’s breaker demolished the first draft’s claim that this negative use is ‘sound/safer’: a low r can arise from unequal coverage or a common-mode systematic with no inadequacy present.

The wrong kernel and the joint inversion

Sze & van der Hilst (2003) ran the largest short-period body-wave inversion to date (839,369 residuals → 29,837 summary rays). Separate-phase inversions disagree badly (up to 13 km for PKPdf), proving topography alone cannot explain the signal. The joint inversion for topography + basal 290 km velocity gives ~3 km. The verification study found the printed PKKP kernel is wrong — in sign and magnitude — against the verified underside-reflection kernel +2η−, surviving five alternative readings. The ~0.2 km excess ellipticity quoted on p. 45 is the initial, untuned model’s value; the published model carries ~0.39 km, so the ‘agrees well with 0.38’ statement spans a factor 1.9. The bootstrap calibration is frequentist and exercised, but the authors themselves declare the blind spot: unsampled cells have small SDs and ‘give misleading sense of a satisfactory result’.

Geodynamic coupling: relation, not prior

Soldati, Boschi & Forte (2012) tied CMB topography to mantle velocity through flow, making dc a deterministic functional of dv. The verification study’s key correction: the geodynamic coupling is not a prior on the model space the method uses — it connects two spaces, making it a relation commitment that sits inside the forward operator and is the property map. It becomes prior information only on a larger space the method never forms. The calibration is none exercised — ‘deterministic point estimate’ was the standard conflation this corpus keeps producing. The synthetic tests that support the headline comparison were built so that the coupling holds exactly, so they measure how much a correct relation helps rather than how well the method resolves the CMB. Four coordinates move between the T and TG procedures (model space, prior, MPR, MDR), not one as the paper states.

The score that looks like a probability

Koelemeijer, Deuss & Ritsema (2017) used Stoneley modes — the sharpest available property map for the CMB — to test whether LLSVPs are dense or light. The model space is three real numbers (R_LL, R_SR, H), not a family of fields; CMB topography is one scalar H multiplying a density-shaped field. The reported ±1 km degree-2 CMB amplitude is five times smaller than the corpus’s standing ±5 km. Its 'probability' P_s (eq. 2) is the weighted fraction of splitting coefficients a grid point fits within their uncertainties. A value of 1 would make the point a member of a bounded-error feasible set; the best value over all Stoneley modes is 0.57, so no point fits all the data, the feasible set is empty, and the reported object is the best-scoring point with its score. Calibration: none exercised. The decisive prior is the post-hoc isostatic sign constraint H < 0, which selects between two classes the seismology leaves degenerate. With ALL available modes the sign of R_LL cannot be determined robustly.

Synthetic ground truth: methods agree, truth loses

Gudmundsson & Clayton (1991) generated traveltime residuals from known mantle models, ran them through the real processing chain, and inverted with two production methods. The methods correlate 0.7 with each other but each correlates worse with the truth — zero or negative at the top and bottom of the lower mantle. An LM-only inversion recovers nearly perfectly, so the problem lies in the data values (contamination), not their spatial distribution (coverage). The variance budget tells the story: the target LM signal is 8% of the total for PcP, dwarfed by upper-mantle leakage and error. Published CMB maps are ‘completely obscured’. This is the keystone that underwrites the 1993 sceptical trio: the parameterisation manufactures structure, and method-to-method agreement is not reliability.

Wavefront healing: the signal that vanishes

Emmerich (1993) computed full-wave synthetics through known CMB topography and found that traveltime residuals stay under 0.5 s for every model — against observed residuals of a few seconds. Short-wavelength topography (< 100 km) is healed out of traveltimes and shifted into amplitudes. The corpus inverts the less informative datum. The verification study’s sharpest finding: a ray-theoretic reading of these synthetics returns a core radius too large by 0.85 to 1.9 km on zero-mean topography — the corpus’s only signed bias, and the same order as its unresolved degree-0 spread (2.62 km, morelli1987 vs tanaka2010). The mechanism is nonlinear: a first-order relation on zero-mean short-wavelength topography returns zero, so Emmerich’s -0.26 s is second order or higher and invisible to every linearised relation in the corpus, ray and Born alike.

The bumps that were gaps

Pulliam & Stark (1993) took the same data, relation and model space as Creager-Jordan and asked: are the ‘bumps’ real or artefacts? Gap deletions of at most 4.68% of the CMB move structure 120–180 degrees away — 23 deleted points under Hawaii produce the largest changes. Rotating the degree-12 reference model under the fixed source-receiver frame manufactures ±2.5 km of mid-Pacific structure on synthetic data whose truth is smooth by construction. Table 2’s AIC prices ALL fitted CMB structure at ~8% of the residual variance. The data ‘give no control over the model in the null-space of the data functionals’. The paper contributes no amplitude: its reference models are disclaimed as topography at the source. Correlation is not reliability — the third independent removal of morelli1987’s agreement device.

The data quality verdict

Rodgers & Wahr (1993) reached the same negative verdict from the data side. The ISC PcP residual rms is 3.81 s — ~21 km of apparent relief, an order of magnitude above every independent bound. The variance decomposition shows random exceeds coherent by 2–3x. The three-way patch fit gives mantle σ_M 0.73 vs CMB σ_CMB 0.15 s — mantle overshadows CMB by ~5x. The degree-2 zonal estimates scatter across signs by phase (PcP 2.59, PKPab -0.80, PKPbc -0.83, PKPdf -6.95 km) with formal errors ~10σ too small. Removing the inferred CMB solution cuts the mantle variance ~33%, so the inversion probably maps more mantle than boundary. The verification study corrected the calibration filing: the variance decomposition is a frequentist-mode AUDIT showing the imputed calibration is not licensed, not a calibration itself.

The confidence envelope

Stark & Hengartner (1993) provided the constructive counterpart: a simultaneous confidence ENVELOPE whose width is governed by the smoothness prior. A degree-20 model reaching 93 km into the mantle changes travel times by only 0.1 s rms, so the data cannot exclude it. The paper's printed envelope magnitudes are not quoted here: the verification found its Tables 3 and 7 inconsistent with its own eq. (65), so only the 93 km / 0.1 s exhibit is used. Whether the ‘bumps’ are real is a joint consequence of the calibration and the smoothness prior, not a fact about Earth — the CMB’s exact instance of the appendix’s S1/R1 collision.

The first calibrated node

Trampert et al. (2004) broke the velocity-density scaling assumption the whole earlier CMB line relied on. By representing seismic data as likelihoods (not single models), including density via normal modes, and carrying mineral-physics uncertainty as pdfs on the property map, they produced the corpus’s first calibrated output. The verification study corrected the calibration type: it is FREQUENTIST (error propagation), not Bayesian — a Bayesian calibration needs an owned probability measure over the model, and there is none. The measure the paper carries is over the property map (mineral-physics pdfs), the seismic constraint is a likelihood, and the only model-side prior is the gravity SET filter. CMB topography is one degree-6 parameter in the SOM. The methodological turning point: after four consecutive ‘none exercised’, a calibration is finally exercised.

The ray limit is exact

Dahlen (2005) derived the finite-frequency Born/Frechet kernel and proved the corpus kernels are its exact ray limit. Eq. (102) recovers D&G 1976; eq. (91)’s dressing factors reproduce -0.146 / +0.250 / +0.052 exactly — a fifth authority on the sze2003 PKKP defect. For least-time PcP the kernel is ZERO at the ray reflection point (a 1200 × 1000 km doughnut at 20 s, scaling with sqrt-period); minimax reflections, including the PKKP underside bounce, peak there instead. The delta-T is a cross-correlation datum (eq. 32) that ISC onset picks are not. Dahlen calls the ISC CMB attempts ‘largely unsuccessful’ by name. A methods paper with no data, no inversion, no prior — its value sits entirely on the relation coordinate.

The degree-0 conflict

Tanaka (2010) used 362 P4KP-PcP differential times — the cleanest body-wave datum, because the differential cancels mantle structure — and reached ±2 km, the smallest amplitude in the body-wave sequence. But the verification study found the paper carries THREE mutually inconsistent statements of its own degree-0 sensitivity, and the degree-0 estimates conflict with morelli1987: +0.12±0.08 vs -2.50±0.20 km, 2.6 km apart with mutually excluding formal errors. Tanaka disclaims his own: ‘the core radius [is] not fundamentally constrained’. The abstract contradicts the body on the solved degrees. The 2 km amplitude is real, but the degree-0 spread (2.62 km across two papers) is the corpus’s sharpest like-for-like discrepancy and remains unresolved.

The genuine posterior

Mosca & Cobden (2012) wrote Bayes explicitly: eq. (6) P(m|d)=k ρ(m) L(m|d), sampled with the Neighbourhood Algorithm, and reported per-node marginal pdfs. CMB topography is an INVERTED model parameter with a uniform prior ±17 km and a stated strong trade-off against density — the first node to carry the boundary inside a sampled posterior rather than correcting it away. The verification study’s key finding: what makes ρ(m) a genuine owned measure is not the word ‘pdf’ but that the paper MARGINALISES AND NORMALISES it — marginalisation is defined on a measure and undefined on a set. The prior is not ‘weak’: a box flat per coefficient is not flat per node. The three-way calibration contrast now stands: NONE, FREQUENTIST, BAYESIAN.

The relation axis tops out

Colombi et al. (2014) climbed to the top of the MDR ladder: Born boundary kernels on spectral-element wavefields, on the corpus’s first measured cross-correlation datum. The mantle exceeds the topography signal by up to 10-fold, and a factor-2 mantle error biases the map up to factor 4 — ‘the mantle is mapped into the CMB’. The joint inversion moves the mantle OUT of the relation INTO the model space, which is soldati2012’s TG move REVERSED. ‘Ray theory does not perform much worse than finite frequency’ — the easy dahlen2005 reconciliation was withdrawn, so the agreement is an open puzzle. The real-data SKSac and PKIKP maps DISAGREE. The corpus’s discrepancy survives the top of the relation axis: a better relation does not buy you out of a prior/data problem.

The coupling prior made tunable

Moulik & Ekstrom (2016) wrote the velocity-density scaling as a quadratic PENALTY with a tunable weight, scanned it across 8 orders of magnitude, and reported the data cannot be fit with a uniform scaling — imposing the coupling degrades 0S2 by ~40%. Where li1991 clamped the scaling hard, this paper made it adjustable and found it partly estimable. The verification study’s biggest find: the dial has FOUR uses — tuned to suppress depth variation in ν, avoid lateral variation in ϴ, give a smooth degree-6 correlation, and match ν ~ 1.5 (a thermal expectation). The headline is now stated BY REGION: tested in the lowermost mantle, tuned in the midmantle. CMB topography is NOT inverted (eq. 4 covers 410/650 only); bounds imported from VLBI. An implicit prior is not always beyond the data’s reach.

The hierarchical posterior

Muir (2022) brought hierarchical Bayes to the body-wave CMB problem. HMC infers the three noise scales AND the two prior scales, and the truncation degree by PSIS-LOO (l′ = 8) — no other corpus paper infers its degree. Built on tanaka2010’s P4KP-PcP data and citing garciaSouriau2000 by name, the posterior gives CMB radius 4.5 km RMS in degrees 1–3, against garciaSouriau2000’s 2.0 ± 0.7 km r.m.s.; the two are the same kind of quantity but not the same scale (wavelengths above 300 km there, degrees 1–3 here), so this is not a like-for-like conflict. The verification study confirmed: genuinely a measure output, the one corpus paper where that filing is right. The ‘two amplitude moves from one cause’ argument was withdrawn: the 0.71% baseline is already hierarchical, and the paper hedges both moves. Physics is a function of the elements jointly, not one-per-coordinate.

The dispute is invisible from body tides

Lau et al. (2017) is the only study in the corpus whose data space is non-seismic: the M2 body tide at 456 GPS stations. CMB topography enters only as a correction from nutation, and five topography models — including li1991, morelli1987, and sze2003 — are tested against the data. The aggregate matched-filter SNR is 0.03 to 0.10. The corpus's amplitude dispute, which spans four decades and three method branches, is undetectable from this vantage point. The study corroborates the seismic density studies in sign but not independently: the model space and property map both consume a seismic v_s field, so the inferences are conditional on a shared structure even though the data are disjoint.
