Part 3a: From Points to Events

Why probability lives on sets, not on points — and why that forces us to think about sigma-algebras.

Part of Bayes, Measure-Theoretically.

Why we need a new language

In Part 2, we solved the switch problem by assigning probabilities to individual hidden states. We wrote things like

\[\mathbb{P}(m=00)=0.4,\qquad \mathbb{P}(m=11)=0.4,\] \[\mathbb{P}(d=0\mid m=00)=0.2,\qquad \mathbb{P}(d=1\mid m=00)=0.8,\]

and everything worked. We built a joint table, normalized columns, and read off posterior probabilities. The arithmetic was simple and unambiguous.

That approach works because the model space has only four points and the data space has only two. But it does not generalize. To see why, we need to ask what happens when the spaces are continuous.

The switch problem works with point probabilities

Let us be explicit about what we did in Part 2. We treated each hidden state \(m\in\M\) as a basic object and attached a probability directly to it:

\[\mathbb{P}(m=00)=0.4, \quad \mathbb{P}(m=01)=0.1, \quad \mathbb{P}(m=10)=0.1, \quad \mathbb{P}(m=11)=0.4.\]

Similarly, each report value \(d\in\D\) received a conditional probability:

\[\mathbb{P}(d=0\mid m=00)=0.2, \qquad \mathbb{P}(d=1\mid m=00)=0.8,\]

and so on for the other three states. From these point probabilities we constructed the joint table \(\mathbb{P}(m,d)\), computed column sums \(\mathbb{P}(d)\), and normalized:

\[\mathbb{P}(m\mid d)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(m,d)}{\mathbb{P}(d)}.\]

Nothing could go wrong, because every point had positive probability. The denominator \(\mathbb{P}(d)\) was always \(0.32\) or \(0.68\), never zero.

Intuition

In a finite world, probability can be attached to individual outcomes. Conditioning is just division. Everything is a table.

A first surprise: what if the hidden state is continuous?

Now imagine a variant of the problem. Instead of two binary switches, the hidden state is a pair of real-valued parameters:

\[m=(m_1,m_2)\in[0,1]^2.\]

The forward map and the noise model are the same in spirit — the reporter observes a noisy function of \(m\) — but the model space is now a continuum.

Assume, as is natural here, that our uncertainty about \(m\) is described by a smooth density over the square — a prior with no lumps of probability concentrated at individual points. We might try to ask the same question as before:

Question

What is the probability that the hidden state equals a specific point \(m=(0.3, 0.7)\)?

The answer is:

\[\mathbb{P}(m=(0.3,0.7))=0.\]

And this is not special to \((0.3,0.7)\). Every individual point in \([0,1]^2\) has probability zero.

At first glance, this seems deeply unsettling. After all, some value of \(m\) must be the true hidden state. How can every possible outcome have probability zero?

Two remarks make this precise. First, a probability measure can have at most countably many atoms — points carrying strictly positive mass. (If uncountably many points each had positive probability, then for some \(n\) uncountably many would carry mass at least \(1/n\), and the total would exceed one.) So on an uncountable space, most points must have probability zero. Second, whether every point has probability zero is a property of the measure, not of the space alone: an uncountable space can perfectly well support atoms (a prior that puts mass \(1/2\) exactly on \((0.3,0.7)\) and spreads the rest smoothly is a legitimate probability measure). A measure with no atoms is called atomless, and our smooth-density prior is atomless — that is why every singleton gets zero. “Continuous model space” and “atomless prior” are different statements, and it is the second one doing the work here.

This does not mean that “nothing happens.” It means that individual outcomes are no longer useful carriers of probability. To be precise: probability was always assigned to measurable sets — a singleton \(\{m\}\) is itself a perfectly good measurable set, in finite and continuous problems alike. The real change is that in the finite problem the singleton masses characterized the whole measure, whereas under an atomless measure every singleton mass is zero, so singleton probabilities no longer distinguish one distribution from another. The information now lives in the probabilities of larger sets.

Key shift

Probability is not about individual outcomes. It is about events — sets of outcomes.

Events as the new basic objects

An event is a set of outcomes, i.e. a subset \(A\subseteq\M\). Instead of asking

\[\text{“What is } \mathbb{P}(m=00)\text{?”}\]

we ask

\[\text{“What is } \mathbb{P}(m\in A)\text{?”}\]

In the switch problem, taking \(A=\{00\}\) gives

\[\mathbb{P}(m\in\{00\})=\mathbb{P}(m=00)=0.4.\]

At first this looks like a pointless relabeling: we took an outcome and put it “in a box” (a singleton set), and declared that this is progress.

But the payoff appears immediately, because once we talk about events as sets, we can combine questions in systematic ways.

Why we need a σ-algebra

Starting with singleton events in the switch problem \(\{00\},\{01\},\{10\},\{11\}\), we are naturally led to form new questions:

Negation. If we can ask “is \(m\in\{00\}\)?”, we should also be able to ask “is \(m\notin\{00\}\)?” In set language, this is the complement:

\[\{00\}^c = \{01,10,11\}.\]

If our probability assignment is to behave coherently, we need

\[\mathbb{P}(\{00\}^c) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(\{00\}) = 0.6.\]

So, starting from singleton events, the demand that we can answer “not” questions forces us to include complements as legitimate events.

Either/or. If we can ask about \(\{00\}\) and \(\{11\}\), we should also be able to ask “is \(m\) either \(00\) or \(11\)?” In set language, this is the union:

\[\{00\}\cup\{11\}=\{00,11\}.\]

For the switch problem:

\[\mathbb{P}(\{00,11\})=\mathbb{P}(\{00\})+\mathbb{P}(\{11\})=0.4+0.4=0.8.\]

And once \(\{00,11\}\) becomes a legitimate event, we should also be able to ask its negation, which introduces its complement:

\[\{00,11\}^c=\{01,10\}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(\{01,10\})=0.2.\]

Iterating. Nothing stops us from repeating this process. Starting with four singletons, we generate all \(2^4=16\) subsets of \(\M\) by taking complements and unions. In the finite case, this process eventually produces every subset, so there is no tension.

But the continuous case is different. There, it turns out that we cannot consistently attach probabilities to every subset of the model space. Instead, we must specify which subsets count as legitimate events — that is, which questions we agree are meaningful to ask and answer.

Intuition

A σ-algebra is a self-contained language of questions about the experiment. It is not an arbitrary definition — it is forced by the logic of combining questions through negation and either/or.

The σ-algebra: formal definition

Let \(\Omega\) be a nonempty set (in our case, \(\Omega=\M\)). A collection \(\mathcal{F}\) of subsets of \(\Omega\) is called a σ-algebra if:

  1. \(\Omega\in\mathcal{F}\) and \(\varnothing\in\mathcal{F}\);
  2. if \(A\in\mathcal{F}\), then its complement \(A^c=\Omega\setminus A\) also belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\);
  3. if \(A_1,A_2,A_3,\dots\in\mathcal{F}\), then their union \(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\in\mathcal{F}\).

Each axiom has a direct interpretation:

  • \(\varnothing\) and \(\Omega\) represent the impossible question (“nothing happens”) and the certain question (“something happens”).
  • Complements correspond to negating a question.
  • Countable unions correspond to asking whether at least one of countably many alternatives occurred.

In the switch problem, the natural σ-algebra is the full power set:

\[\SigmaM = 2^{\M} = \{\text{all subsets of } \M\}.\]

This has \(16\) elements: the empty set, four singletons, six pairs, four triples, and \(\M\) itself. Every subset is an event.

In continuous spaces, the full power set is no longer a workable choice — not because no measure could ever live on it (a point mass \(\delta_x\) is happily defined on every subset), but because the measures we actually want are ruled out. If we ask for the usual atomless, translation-invariant notion of “area” on \([0,1]^2\) (or length on \(\mathbb{R}\)), then it is impossible to extend it to all subsets while keeping countable additivity — this is the content of the classical Vitali construction of a non-measurable set. The mainstream resolution is to shrink the domain: the natural choice is the Borel σ-algebra, which is the smallest σ-algebra containing all open sets. This is the σ-algebra we use when the model space is, say, \([0,1]^2\).

The probability measure: the object that answers the questions

Once we know which questions we are allowed to ask (the σ-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\)), we still need something that provides numerical answers.

A probability measure is a function

\[\mu:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]\]

satisfying:

  1. \(\mu(\Omega)=1\);
  2. for any sequence of pairwise disjoint sets \(A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{F}\), \[\mu\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_n).\]

The first axiom says that total probability is one. The second says that probabilities of mutually exclusive events add — the “either/or” principle, extended to countably many alternatives.

In the switch problem, the prior is a probability measure \(\muMzero:\SigmaM\to[0,1]\). On singleton events:

\[\muMzero(\{00\})=0.4, \quad \muMzero(\{01\})=0.1, \quad \muMzero(\{10\})=0.1, \quad \muMzero(\{11\})=0.4.\]

Since every event in \(\SigmaM\) is a union of singletons, these four numbers determine the entire measure. For example:

\[\muMzero(\{00,11\})=\muMzero(\{00\})+\muMzero(\{11\})=0.8,\] \[\muMzero(\{01,10\})=\muMzero(\{01\})+\muMzero(\{10\})=0.2.\]

The probability space

The triple \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\) is the modern mathematical model of a probabilistic experiment. It captures:

  • \(\Omega\): the universe — all possible outcomes.
  • \(\mathcal{F}\): the questions we allow ourselves to ask (events).
  • \(\mu\): the rule that assigns answers to those questions.

(This triple, and the axioms behind it, are Kolmogorov’s 1933 axiomatization of probability [Kol33] — the moment probability theory became a branch of measure theory. Every modern textbook, e.g. Kallenberg [Kal21], begins exactly here.)

For the switch problem, the model probability space is \((\M,\SigmaM,\muMzero)\) and the data probability space is \((\D,\SigmaD,\muD)\), where \(\SigmaD=2^{\D}\).

Why this matters

Conditioning — the heart of Bayesian inference — will turn out to be fundamentally about restricting or refining the collection of questions \(\mathcal{F}\), rather than about fixing individual outcomes. This viewpoint is the key to avoiding paradoxes that arise when one tries to condition directly on events of probability zero.

What we have gained

By re-expressing the prior as a measure on events rather than a table of point probabilities, we have not changed any numbers. The prior measure \(\muMzero\) contains exactly the same information as the prior table from Part 2.

What we have gained is a language that survives when the spaces are continuous. The measure \(\muMzero\) assigns probabilities to events, not to points, and this works equally well whether \(\M\) has four elements or uncountably many.

In Part 3b, we turn to conditioning — the operation at the heart of Bayesian inference. We will see that conditioning works perfectly in the finite switch problem, works for positive-measure sets in continuous spaces, but breaks down for individual observations in continuous spaces. Understanding this breakdown is essential for appreciating why the measure-theoretic formulation is not merely elegant, but necessary.
