Part 3b: Conditioning and Its Limits

Conditioning works in the finite case, works on positive-measure sets, and breaks on points.

Conditioning: the operation at the heart of Bayes

In Part 3a, we saw why probability must be assigned to events rather than individual outcomes. Now we turn to conditioning — the operation that updates our beliefs in light of observed data.

Conditioning is what Bayesian inference is. The posterior is a conditional distribution. So we need to understand conditioning deeply, including where it works, where it fails, and why.

Conditioning in the switch problem (discrete case)

In the switch problem, conditioning is immediate, natural, and unambiguous. For any two events \(A\in\SigmaM\) and \(B\in\SigmaD\) with \(\mathbb{P}(B)>0\), the conditional probability is defined by the elementary ratio:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}.\]

This formula simply says: restrict the universe to \(B\), then renormalize to make total probability one.

Concretely, from Part 2 we know the joint probabilities. One point of care: the event \(\{00\}\) lives in the model space \(\M\), while \(\{0\}\) lives in the data space \(\D\) — they are subsets of different spaces, so writing \(\{00\}\cap\{0\}\) would be ill-typed. The joint event “\(M=00\) and \(D=0\)” lives in the product space \(\M\times\D\), where the joint measure \(\Pi\) assigns:

\[\Pi\bigl(\{00\}\times\{0\}\bigr)=0.08, \qquad \muD\bigl(\{0\}\bigr)=0.32.\]

So

\[\mathbb{P}\bigl(M=00\mid D=0\bigr)=\frac{\Pi(\{00\}\times\{0\})}{\muD(\{0\})}=\frac{0.08}{0.32}=\frac{1}{4}.\]

This is just column normalization. The denominator \(\muD(\{0\})=0.32\) is positive, so the division is well-defined. The same works for the data value \(1\):

\[\mathbb{P}\bigl(M=00\mid D=1\bigr)=\frac{\Pi(\{00\}\times\{1\})}{\muD(\{1\})}=\frac{0.32}{0.68}=\frac{8}{17}.\]

(Equivalently, one can introduce an underlying sample space \(\Omega\) carrying random variables \(M:\Omega\to\M\) and \(D:\Omega\to\D\), and intersect the pullback events \(M^{-1}(\{00\})\cap D^{-1}(\{0\})\), which do live in the same space. The two formulations agree; we will use whichever is clearer in context.)

Intuition

Conditioning via the ratio is completely routine here because both possible data values have positive predictive probability (\(0.32\) and \(0.68\)), so conditioning on either one is well-defined. This is typical of small discrete problems, though not automatic: even a finite space can contain events of probability zero, and the ratio is undefined for those. What discreteness buys you in practice is that the data values you actually observe have positive probability — so the elementary ratio never lets you down.

What if the data space were continuous?

Now imagine a variant of the switch problem where the reporter outputs a real-valued measurement \(d\in\mathbb{R}\) rather than a binary report. Assume the predictive data measure \(\muD\) is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, so it has a density — a smooth curve over the real line. (A real-valued observable need not have a density in general: its distribution can have atoms or even a singular continuous part. We assume a density here because it is the case that exhibits the difficulty most cleanly.)

Conditioning on a region \(B\) with \(\muD(B)>0\) still works perfectly. For example, if we observe that \(d\in[0.3, 0.7]\), we can condition on this interval:

\[\mathbb{P}(m\in A\mid d\in[0.3,0.7])=\frac{\mathbb{P}(m\in A, d\in[0.3,0.7])}{\mathbb{P}(d\in[0.3,0.7])}.\]

The denominator is the probability mass in the interval, which is positive. “Restrict and renormalize” still applies. No problem.

The Achilles' heel: conditioning on a single observation

In practice, we do not observe that \(d\in[0.3,0.7]\). We observe a specific value \(d_{\mathrm{obs}}\). In continuous spaces, a single point has zero probability:

\[\muD(\{d_{\mathrm{obs}}\})=0.\]

If we try to apply the ratio formula, we get:

\[\mathbb{P}(m\in A\mid d=d_{\mathrm{obs}})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(m\in A, d=d_{\mathrm{obs}})}{\mathbb{P}(d=d_{\mathrm{obs}})}=\frac{0}{0}.\]

This is not a technical inconvenience. It is a genuine breakdown of the definition. The elementary conditioning rule no longer produces a number.

The problem

Conditioning on events only works when the conditioning event has positive probability. As soon as we try to condition on a set that is “too small” — a single point in a continuous space — the idea of “restrict and renormalize” collapses. There is no mass to renormalize.

The “shrink the band” instinct and the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox

A natural instinct arises: Fine, I cannot condition directly on the point. Let me instead condition on a small interval around it, and then shrink the interval.

Indeed, conditioning on \(d\in[d_{\mathrm{obs}}-\varepsilon, d_{\mathrm{obs}}+\varepsilon]\) works perfectly well for every \(\varepsilon>0\). So why not take the limit \(\varepsilon\to 0\)?

This instinct is reasonable — but it hides a subtle trap. Different ways of shrinking toward the point can lead to different limiting answers.

To see this concretely, consider a two-dimensional example. Suppose the hidden state is \((m_1,m_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) with a smooth joint density \(\rho(m_1,m_2)\), and we want to condition on \(m_1=2\). The set \(S=\{(m_1,m_2):m_1=2\}\) is a vertical line — a one-dimensional object in a two-dimensional space — and has zero probability.

First approach: vertical strips. Condition on \(m_1\in[2-\varepsilon, 2+\varepsilon]\) and let \(\varepsilon\to 0\). This produces a conditional density proportional to \(\rho(2, m_2)\) along the line.

Derivation

The conditional density of \(m_2\) given the strip \(m_1\in[2-\varepsilon,\,2+\varepsilon]\) is:

\[p_\varepsilon(m_2)=\frac{\int_{2-\varepsilon}^{2+\varepsilon}\rho(m_1,m_2)\,dm_1}{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{2-\varepsilon}^{2+\varepsilon}\rho(m_1,m_2')\,dm_1\,dm_2'}.\]

As \(\varepsilon\to 0\), by continuity of \(\rho\):

\[\int_{2-\varepsilon}^{2+\varepsilon}\rho(m_1,m_2)\,dm_1\;\approx\;2\varepsilon\,\rho(2,m_2).\]

The factor \(2\varepsilon\) cancels between numerator and denominator, leaving:

\[p_\varepsilon(m_2)\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{\rho(2,m_2)}{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(2,m_2')\,dm_2'}\;\propto\;\rho(2,m_2).\]

Second approach: tilted tubes. Condition on \(|m_1-2|<\varepsilon|m_2|\) and let \(\varepsilon\to 0\). This also collapses onto the same line, but produces a conditional density proportional to \(|m_2|\,\rho(2, m_2)\).

Derivation

Now the conditioning set is \(|m_1-2|<\varepsilon|m_2|\), so the strip width depends on \(m_2\). The conditional density of \(m_2\) is:

\[p_\varepsilon(m_2)=\frac{\int_{2-\varepsilon|m_2|}^{2+\varepsilon|m_2|}\rho(m_1,m_2)\,dm_1}{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{2-\varepsilon|m_2'|}^{2+\varepsilon|m_2'|}\rho(m_1,m_2')\,dm_1\,dm_2'}.\]

As \(\varepsilon\to 0\), by continuity of \(\rho\):

\[\int_{2-\varepsilon|m_2|}^{2+\varepsilon|m_2|}\rho(m_1,m_2)\,dm_1\;\approx\;2\varepsilon\,|m_2|\,\rho(2,m_2).\]

The factor \(2\varepsilon\) cancels, but the \(|m_2|\) factor remains — it depends on the integration variable and does not cancel:

\[p_\varepsilon(m_2)\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{|m_2|\,\rho(2,m_2)}{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|m_2'|\,\rho(2,m_2')\,dm_2'}\;\propto\;|m_2|\,\rho(2,m_2).\]

These are different probability measures on the same line. Both limits condition on the same measure-zero set, yet they produce different answers. The result depends on how the limit is taken — on the geometry of the shrinking sets — and probability theory alone does not specify which geometry to use.

This is the Borel–Kolmogorov (BK) paradox: the conditional distribution on a measure-zero set is not uniquely determined by the probability model. It depends on additional geometric structure that is not part of the model.

Why this matters for Bayesian inversion

In Bayesian inverse problems, we always condition on observed data. If the data space is continuous, this is almost always a measure-zero event. Does this mean that all of Bayesian inference is broken?

No. The resolution is that a Bayesian inverse problem provides us with more structure than just “a set of measure zero.” It provides us with a random variable \(D\) — the data — and conditioning on \(D\) (via its σ-algebra) gives a canonical answer that does not depend on any arbitrary geometric choice.

The key insight is this:

The resolution

Instead of conditioning on a set \(\{d=d_{\mathrm{obs}}\}\) of probability zero, we condition on the σ-algebra generated by the random variable \(D\). This σ-algebra encodes the information revealed by observing \(D\), and conditioning on it produces a conditional distribution that is canonical up to \(\muD\)-null sets: it is determined for \(\muD\)-almost every data value, with no geometric choices required. What it does not do is single out a value at one specific probability-zero datum — evaluating there requires choosing a regular version of the conditional, a point we return to carefully in Parts 3c and 3d.

In the switch problem, this distinction is invisible because the data space is finite and every point has positive probability. But the machinery we build now — conditioning on σ-algebras — is exactly what will make the continuous case work.
