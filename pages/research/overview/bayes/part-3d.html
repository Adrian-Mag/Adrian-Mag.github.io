Part 3d: Disintegration

From conditional expectation to conditional probability measures.

Part of Bayes, Measure-Theoretically.

« Overview 1 The Game 2 High-School Solution 3a Points → Events 3b Conditioning 3c Cond. Expectation 3d Disintegration 3e Bayesian Inversion 3f Back to Finite 4 Summary

The loose end from conditional expectation

In Part 3c, conditional expectation gave us, for each model event \(A\in\SigmaM\), a function

\[\mathbb{P}(M\in A\mid D)=k_A(D),\]

where \(k_A:\D\to[0,1]\) is measurable. Evaluating at observed data \(d\) gives the conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}(M\in A\mid D=d)=k_A(d)\).

This is a powerful result, but it answers only one question at a time. Pick an event \(A\), get a function of \(d\). Pick another event \(A'\), get another function. Bayesian inference wants more than that: after observing \(d\), we want a full posterior distribution on \(\M\) — a probability measure that can answer every model-event question simultaneously.

One event is not enough: bundling into a measure

Here is the key idea. For each fixed \(d\), we have a collection of numbers \(k_A(d)\), one for each \(A\in\SigmaM\). What if the assignment \(A\mapsto k_A(d)\) behaves like a probability measure on \(\M\)? Then for each data value \(d\), we would have a full probability measure — not just a collection of isolated conditional probabilities, but a coherent distribution that assigns probabilities to all model events at once.

(The object we are about to assemble is a Markov kernel, and the theorem that guarantees the bundling works is the disintegration theorem. The program of this part — treating conditioning as disintegration rather than as a ratio of probabilities — follows Chang and Pollard’s classic account [CP97]; the general theory is in Kallenberg [Kal21].)

Let us name this object:

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A):=k_A(d).\]

The notation is deliberate: \(\mu_{\M\mid D=d}\) is a measure (in \(A\)) that depends on the data value \(d\). For each \(d\), it is a probability measure on \((\M,\SigmaM)\). For each \(A\), the map \(d\mapsto\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\) is measurable (it is just \(k_A(d)\), which we already know is measurable).

The conceptual ladder

Conditional expectation gives \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid D)=k_A(D)\) for one event \(A\). Doob–Dynkin lets us write \(d\mapsto k_A(d)\). Now flip the perspective: fix \(d\) and vary \(A\). If \(A\mapsto k_A(d)\) is a measure, we have \(\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\). That is disintegration: conditional expectation, bundled over all events.

When this bundling works — when \(A\mapsto k_A(d)\) is genuinely a probability measure for each \(d\) — we call the resulting object a conditional distribution or posterior kernel. The theorem guaranteeing that it works, under good measurable-space assumptions (e.g. standard Borel spaces), is the disintegration theorem. A point of precision about what is and is not pinned down: a kernel is ordinarily presented as a function defined for every \(d\) — one can always fill in arbitrary probability measures on an exceptional null set to achieve this. What is determined by the joint law is the kernel's equivalence class: two valid kernels must agree for \(\muD\)-almost every \(d\), but may differ freely on a \(\muD\)-null set. So the accurate slogan is not “defined almost everywhere” but “defined everywhere, unique almost everywhere.”

The consistency identity

Where does the joint law enter? Recall the defining property of conditional expectation from Part 3c: for any data event \(B\in\SigmaD\),

\[\int_{D^{-1}(B)} \mathbb{P}(M\in A\mid D)\,d\mathbb{P} = \mathbb{P}(M\in A,\; D\in B).\]

By Doob–Dynkin, \(\mathbb{P}(M\in A\mid D)=k_A(D)=\mu_{\M\mid D=D(\omega)}(A)\). The left side is an integral over outcomes \(\omega\), but since the integrand depends on \(\omega\) only through \(D(\omega)\), we can push it down to data space. The pushforward of \(\mathbb{P}\) under \(D\) is the predictive data measure \(\muD\), so:

\[\mathbb{P}(M\in A,\; D\in B) = \int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd).\]

The left side is the joint probability of \((A,B)\). Writing \(\Pi\) for the joint measure on \(\M\times\D\), this becomes:

\[\boxed{\;\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd)\;}\]
The aha

The disintegration identity is not a new axiom lowered from the rafters. It is the defining property of conditional expectation, rewritten on data space via Doob–Dynkin. Disintegration is conditional expectation, bundled over all events and pushed onto the observed variable.

The same trick in the other direction

We just bundled the model-side conditional probabilities into a posterior kernel. The same logic works in the opposite direction — from model to data.

For each data event \(B\in\SigmaD\), conditional expectation gives a function of the model:

\[\mathbb{P}(D\in B\mid M)=\ell_B(M),\]

where \(\ell_B:\M\to[0,1]\) is measurable. By the same bundling move, fix \(m\) and vary \(B\): if \(B\mapsto \ell_B(m)\) is a probability measure on \(\D\), we name it

\[L(m,B):=\ell_B(m)=\mathbb{P}(D\in B\mid M=m).\]

This is the observation kernel. For each fixed \(m\), \(L(m,\cdot)\) is a probability measure on \((\D,\SigmaD)\) — it tells us how data is distributed when the model is \(m\).

The same consistency argument gives the forward disintegration:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm),\]

where \(\muMzero\) is the prior — the marginal of \(\Pi\) on the model space, obtained by integrating out the data: \(\muMzero(A)=\Pi(A\times\D)\). This says: the joint probability of \((A,B)\) decomposes as the integral of the observation kernel \(L(m,B)\), weighted by the prior, over all model states in \(A\).

The reverse ladder

\(\mathbb{P}(B\mid M)=\ell_B(M)\) gives \(m\mapsto \ell_B(m)\) via Doob–Dynkin. Flip perspective: fix \(m\), vary \(B\). If \(B\mapsto \ell_B(m)\) is a measure, we have \(L(m,B)\). The same consistency identity then gives \(\Pi(A\times B)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm)\).

Two directions, one mechanism

If a joint law \(\Pi\) is already given, then \(L(m,\cdot)\) is the disintegration of \(\Pi\) with respect to the model marginal, and \(\mu_{\M\mid D=d}\) is the disintegration with respect to the data marginal. In Bayesian inversion, we often specify the prior and the observation kernel first, and thereby build the joint law. Either way, the same mechanism produces both kernels.

The finite switch problem as a discovery lab

Let us see the bundling operation concretely. Recall the setup from Part 1: \(\M=\{00,01,10,11\}\) with prior \(\muMzero\), and \(\D=\{0,1\}\). The joint table is:

\(d=0\) \(d=1\) \(\muMzero(\{m\})\)
000.080.320.40
010.080.020.10
100.080.020.10
110.080.320.40
\(\muD(\{d\})\)0.320.681

Each cell is \(\Pi(\{m\}\times\{d\})\). The last column gives row sums (the prior); the last row gives column sums (the predictive data measure).

Columns → posterior kernel

Fix a data value, say \(d=1\). The column \(\{0.32, 0.02, 0.02, 0.32\}\) sums to \(\muD(\{1\})=0.68\). Normalizing gives

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{m\})=\frac{\Pi(\{m\}\times\{1\})}{\muD(\{1\})}.\]

This is the posterior distribution over models given \(d=1\): \(\{0.47, 0.03, 0.03, 0.47\}\). Repeating for \(d=0\) gives the posterior for the other observation. The column-normalization operation is the bundling: for each \(d\), it produces a full probability measure on \(\M\).

Rows → observation kernel

Now fix a model value, say \(m=00\). The row \(\{0.08, 0.32\}\) sums to \(\muMzero(\{00\})=0.40\). Normalizing gives

\[L(00,\{d\})=\frac{\Pi(\{00\}\times\{d\})}{\muMzero(\{00\})}.\]

This is the data distribution given \(m=00\): \(\{0.2, 0.8\}\). Repeating for each \(m\) gives the observation kernel. The row-normalization operation is the same bundling in the other direction. (We reserve the word likelihood for the density \(L(d\mid m)\) of Part 3e, or for the function \(m\mapsto L(d\mid m)\) once the data is fixed; the measure-valued object \(L(m,\cdot)\) is the observation kernel.)

Two ways of reading the same table

Columns (normalize by column sum) \(\Rightarrow\) posterior kernel \(\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\).
Rows (normalize by row sum) \(\Rightarrow\) observation kernel \(L(m,B)\).
Both are disintegrations of the same joint table.

Two decompositions of the same joint law

We now have two ways to decompose the same joint measure \(\Pi\) on \(\M\times\D\):

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd) \qquad\text{(posterior: model given data)},\] \[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm) \qquad\text{(observation kernel: data given model)}.\]

The first disintegrates with respect to the predictive data measure \(\muD\) and produces the posterior kernel. The second disintegrates with respect to the prior \(\muMzero\) and produces the observation kernel. Both are decompositions of the same joint measure — they are two sides of the same coin.

In the finite table, this is simply the observation that you can read the table by columns or by rows. In general spaces, the disintegration theorem guarantees that both decompositions exist and are unique (almost everywhere) under standard conditions.

We do not yet set these two expressions equal. That will be the subject of Part 3e, where comparing them will give us Bayes' formula. For now, the point is that the joint measure admits two disintegrations — one in each direction — and both arise from the same conditional-expectation mechanism we developed in Part 3c.

A warning about evaluation

Conditional expectation does not magically assign a unique value at every zero-probability data point. It gives a version determined only up to \(\muD\)-null sets. To evaluate at a specific observed \(\dobs\), one often needs additional structure — such as a likelihood density, continuity, a chosen regular conditional distribution, or a geometric formula such as coarea in noiseless inverse problems.

This is the anti-Borel–Kolmogorov charm: it keeps the exposition honest. The posterior kernel can be chosen everywhere but is unique only \(\muD\)-a.e., and pointwise evaluation at \(\dobs\) requires regularity that must be checked, not assumed.

Summary

Conditional expectation gives conditional probabilities one event at a time. Disintegration bundles them into full probability measures. The joint law \(\Pi\) admits two disintegrations: the posterior kernel \(\mu_{\M\mid D=d}\) (disintegration with respect to \(\muD\)) and the observation kernel \(L\) (disintegration with respect to \(\muMzero\)). Comparing them will give us Bayes' formula.
