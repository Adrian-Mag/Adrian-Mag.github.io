Part 3f: Back to Finite Land

Applying the integral Bayes formula to the finite switch problem — bringing everything home.

Part of Bayes, Measure-Theoretically.

« Overview 1 The Game 2 High-School Solution 3a Points → Events 3b Conditioning 3c Cond. Expectation 3d Disintegration 3e Bayesian Inversion 3f Back to Finite 4 Summary

The integral Bayes formula, applied to the switch problem

In Part 3e, we derived the measure-theoretic Bayes formula:

\[\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

This is an identity between two disintegrations of the same joint law. It works in both finite and continuous spaces. Now we apply it to the finite switch problem — the same problem we started with in Part 1 and solved with ratios in Part 2.

The goal is to see that the measure-theoretic formula, when specialized to finite spaces, reproduces exactly the elementary Bayes' rule. The integrals become sums, the kernels become conditional probability tables, and the posterior kernel becomes the normalized column.

Recall the setup

The model space is \(\M=\{00,01,10,11\}\) with prior \(\muMzero\). The data space is \(\D=\{0,1\}\). The observation kernel is \(L(m,B)=\mathbb{P}(D\in B\mid M=m)\).

The joint measure is:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

In the finite case, the integral is a sum:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\sum_{m\in A} L(m,B)\,\muMzero(\{m\}).\]

The predictive data measure is:

\[\muD(B)=\Pi(\M\times B)=\sum_{m\in\M} L(m,B)\,\muMzero(\{m\}).\]

We computed these in Part 3d:

\[\muD(\{0\})=0.32, \qquad \muD(\{1\})=0.68.\]

Solving for the posterior kernel

The posterior-side disintegration gives:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd).\]

Because \(\D=\{0,1\}\), we can take \(B=\{d\}\) for each \(d\in\D\). The integral becomes a single term:

\[\Pi(A\times\{d\})=\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(\{d\}).\]

Since \(\muD(\{0\})=0.32>0\) and \(\muD(\{1\})=0.68>0\), we may divide:

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)=\frac{\Pi(A\times\{d\})}{\muD(\{d\})}=\frac{\int_A L(m,\{d\})\,\muMzero(dm)}{\int_{\M} L(m,\{d\})\,\muMzero(dm)}.\]
The formula in finite land

This is the integral Bayes formula applied to the finite case. The numerator is an integral over \(A\) — in finite land, a weighted sum. The denominator is the evidence \(Z(d)\) — also a sum. The ratio is exactly the normalized column of the joint table.

Computing the posterior probabilities

Case \(d=0\): the reporter says “0”

For \(A=\{00\}\):

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=0}(\{00\})=\frac{L(00,\{0\})\,\muMzero(\{00\})}{\muD(\{0\})}=\frac{0.2\cdot 0.4}{0.32}=\frac{0.08}{0.32}=\frac{1}{4}.\]

By symmetry, the same holds for all model states:

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=0}(\{00\})=\mu_{\M\mid D=0}(\{01\})=\mu_{\M\mid D=0}(\{10\})=\mu_{\M\mid D=0}(\{11\})=\frac{1}{4}.\]

When the reporter says “0”, the posterior is uniform — it coincides with the prior. The observation \(d=0\) is equally likely under every model state (each has \(L(m,\{0\})=0.2\)), so the likelihood factor cancels upon normalization and the posterior reduces to the prior.

Case \(d=1\): the reporter says “1”

For \(A=\{00\}\):

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{00\})=\frac{L(00,\{1\})\,\muMzero(\{00\})}{\muD(\{1\})}=\frac{0.8\cdot 0.4}{0.68}=\frac{0.32}{0.68}=\frac{8}{17}.\]

For \(A=\{11\}\):

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{11\})=\frac{L(11,\{1\})\,\muMzero(\{11\})}{\muD(\{1\})}=\frac{0.8\cdot 0.4}{0.68}=\frac{8}{17}.\]

For \(A=\{01\}\):

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{01\})=\frac{L(01,\{1\})\,\muMzero(\{01\})}{\muD(\{1\})}=\frac{0.2\cdot 0.1}{0.68}=\frac{0.02}{0.68}=\frac{1}{34}.\]

By symmetry, \(\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{10\})=\frac{1}{34}\) as well.

So when the reporter says “1”, the posterior concentrates on the matching states \(\{00,11\}\):

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{00\})=\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{11\})=\frac{8}{17}\approx 0.471,\] \[\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{01\})=\mu_{\M\mid D=1}(\{10\})=\frac{1}{34}\approx 0.029.\]

Plugging in the actual observation

We have now constructed the full posterior kernel \(d\longmapsto \mu_{\M\mid D=d}\). Only at this point do we use the actual observed report. If the reporter announces \(\dobs\in\D\), then the posterior measure on the model space is:

\[\mu^{\mathrm{post}}_{\M}(\cdot)=\mu_{\M\mid D=\dobs}(\cdot).\]
Intuition

The observation selects a measure from the posterior kernel. It does not create the update rule from scratch. The kernel was already constructed by disintegration; the observation merely picks the appropriate slice.

The full circle

Let us trace the journey:

  1. Part 1: We posed the switch problem — a finite game with hidden states and a noisy reporter.
  2. Part 2: We solved it with the elementary ratio formula \(\mathbb{P}(m\mid d)=\mathbb{P}(m,d)/\mathbb{P}(d)\) — column normalization of the joint table.
  3. Parts 3a–3b: We saw that the elementary formula breaks when conditioning events have probability zero (Borel–Kolmogorov paradox).
  4. Part 3c: We defined conditional expectation via integrals, avoiding division by zero. Conditional probability falls out as conditional expectation of indicator functions.
  5. Part 3d: We bundled eventwise conditional probabilities into conditional probability measures via disintegration.
  6. Part 3e: We derived Bayes' formula as the equality of two disintegrations of the same joint law. The familiar density form appeared as a special case.
  7. Part 3f: We applied the integral formula back to the finite switch problem. The integrals became sums, the kernels became tables, and the answer is exactly the same as in Part 2.
The payoff

The measure-theoretic machinery — conditional expectation, disintegration, posterior kernels — reproduces the elementary answer in finite land, but it also works in continuous spaces where the elementary formula breaks down. We climbed to infinite land, built a more general framework, and descended back to find that the familiar answer was there all along.

The story ends where it began — in finite land — but we now carry the tools to go beyond it.
