Part 3e: Bayesian Inversion

Bayes' formula as the equality of two disintegrations of the same joint law.

Part of Bayes, Measure-Theoretically.

The inversion problem

In Part 3d, we saw that the joint law \(\Pi\) on \(\M\times\D\) admits two disintegrations:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd) \qquad\text{(posterior: model given data)},\] \[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm) \qquad\text{(observation kernel: data given model)}.\]

In Bayesian inversion, we usually specify the problem in the opposite direction from what we want. We do not start by knowing the posterior. We start with:

  1. A prior on models, \(\muMzero(dm)\).
  2. A data-given-model kernel, \(L(m,B)=\mathbb{P}(D\in B\mid M=m)\).

The kernel \(L\) says: if the model takes the value \(m\), then the data is distributed according to the measure \(L(m,\cdot)\) on \(\D\). This is also a conditional probability measure, but in the forward direction — from model to data.

From these two ingredients, the joint law is determined:

\[\Pi(dm,dd)=L(m,dd)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

For model event \(A\) and data event \(B\):

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

This is the forward disintegration — the generative direction. But the same joint law also admits the posterior disintegration:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd).\]

Since both expressions equal \(\Pi(A\times B)\), we obtain the central measure-theoretic Bayes formula:

\[\boxed{\;\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,\muD(dd)=\int_A L(m,B)\,\muMzero(dm)\;}\]
Bayes as an identity

This is not the density formula. Not yet “posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior.” This is Bayes as an identity between two ways of decomposing the same joint probability. The left side disintegrates with respect to the data marginal; the right side disintegrates with respect to the model marginal. Both describe the same joint measure.

The density form as a special case

The familiar formula “posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior” appears only when the data-given-model kernel has a density with respect to a reference measure.

Assume there exists a σ-finite reference measure \(\lambda\) on \((\D,\SigmaD)\) such that \(L(m,\cdot)\ll\lambda\) for \(\muMzero\)-a.e.\(m\). Then for each such \(m\) the Radon–Nikodým derivative

\[L(d\mid m):=\frac{dL(m,\cdot)}{d\lambda}(d)\]

exists \(\lambda\)-a.e. One further point deserves honesty: the derivatives are defined separately for each \(m\), and choosing them so that \((m,d)\mapsto L(d\mid m)\) is jointly measurable is a genuine (if standard) extra step. It is guaranteed under the assumptions we work with throughout — \(\lambda\) σ-finite, the data σ-algebra countably generated (e.g. \(\D\) a standard Borel space), and \(L\) a measurable kernel — but it is not automatic on arbitrary measurable spaces. Granting joint measurability, the joint law admits the density representation:

\[\Pi(dm,dd)=L(d\mid m)\,\lambda(dd)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

Identification of the data marginal

Integrating the joint density over \(\M\) yields:

\[\muD(dd)=Z(d)\,\lambda(dd), \qquad Z(d):=\int_{\M} L(d\mid m)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

The function \(Z(d)\) is the evidence (or marginal likelihood) — the same quantity that appeared as the denominator in our finite example.

Comparing the two disintegrations

Substituting the density form of \(\muD\) into the posterior disintegration:

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B \mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,Z(d)\,\lambda(dd).\]

On the other hand, combining the forward disintegration with the density representation of \(L(m,\cdot)\):

\[\Pi(A\times B)=\int_B\left(\int_A L(d\mid m)\,\muMzero(dm)\right)\lambda(dd).\]

Since these two expressions represent the same measure for all measurable \(B\), it follows that for \(\lambda\)-a.e.\(d\):

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(A)\,Z(d)=\int_A L(d\mid m)\,\muMzero(dm), \qquad A\in\SigmaM.\]

(A technical footnote: for each fixed \(A\) this equality holds outside an \(A\)-dependent \(\lambda\)-null set. To get one exceptional null set that works for all \(A\) simultaneously — which is what the displayed statement asserts — one runs the argument over a countable generating class of model events and extends by a monotone-class argument. This is available when \(\SigmaM\) is countably generated, as it is for standard Borel model spaces. Alternatively, one can simply define the kernel by the ratio formula below wherever \(Z(d)>0\) and verify directly that it satisfies the disintegration identity.)

Bayes' relation in density form

If \(Z(d)\in(0,\infty)\) for \(\muD\)-a.e.\(d\), then the posterior measure is uniquely determined as:

\[\boxed{\;\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(dm)=\frac{L(d\mid m)\,\muMzero(dm)}{Z(d)}\;}\]

Equivalently, before normalization:

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(dm)\;\propto\;L(d\mid m)\,\muMzero(dm).\]
Key point

The familiar formula “posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior” is not the foundation. It is the coordinate/density version of the deeper measure identity. It is valid only when suitable Radon–Nikodým derivatives exist.

Remark on reference measures

The likelihood \(L(d\mid m)\) depends on the choice of reference measure \(\lambda\) and is therefore not an invariant object. The posterior kernel \(\mu_{\M\mid D=d}\), by contrast, is determined up to \(\muD\)-null sets and does not depend on any reference measure. Even in the total absence of a dominating reference, the posterior kernel still exists as a regular conditional distribution provided the spaces are well-behaved — standard Borel spaces (Polish spaces with their Borel σ-algebras, which cover every finite, Euclidean, and separable Banach setting we care about) are the usual sufficient condition. On truly pathological measurable spaces, regular conditional distributions can fail to exist. Within the standard-Borel world: existence holds, uniqueness holds up to \(\muD\)-null sets, and only the simple density formula needs the dominating reference measure.

The likelihood from forward models with additive noise

In many inverse problems, the data-generating mechanism has a specific structure: a deterministic forward map perturbed by additive noise. We now show how the observation kernel arises naturally in this setting.

Setup

Assume:

  • The data space is \(\D=\mathbb{R}^{N_d}\) with its Borel σ-algebra. (The additive structure below needs a vector space with measurable addition; for this section we simply take Euclidean data space, which is the practically relevant case.)
  • \(M\sim\muMzero\) on \((\M,\SigmaM)\) — the prior;
  • \(\eta\sim\mu_\eta\) on \((\D,\SigmaD)\) — the noise distribution;
  • \(M\) and \(\eta\) are independent;
  • The data is generated by \(D=G(M)+\eta\), where \(G:\M\to\D\) is a measurable forward map.

The joint law of \((M,D)\) is the pushforward of the product measure \(\muMzero\otimes\mu_\eta\) under the map \(\Phi(m,\xi)=(m,\,G(m)+\xi)\):

\[\Pi=\Phi_\#(\muMzero\otimes\mu_\eta).\]

The data-given-model kernel

Fixing \(M=m\), the forward map value \(G(M)\) becomes the deterministic element \(G(m)\). The only remaining randomness in \(D\) comes from the noise \(\eta\). Hence:

\[D\mid(M=m)\;\overset{d}{=}\;G(m)+\eta.\]

Translating the event \(\{D\in B\}\) back to an event in terms of \(\eta\):

\[\{D\in B\}=\{G(m)+\eta\in B\}=\{\eta\in B-G(m)\},\]

where \(B-G(m):=\{\xi\in\D:\xi+G(m)\in B\}\). Taking probabilities under the noise law yields:

\[L(m,B)=\mu_\eta\!\bigl(B-G(m)\bigr), \qquad B\in\SigmaD.\]

In pushforward notation: \(L(m,\cdot)=(T_{G(m)})_\#\mu_\eta\), where \(T_y(\xi)=\xi+y\). The data-given-model kernel is simply the noise distribution shifted by the forward response.

The likelihood as a shifted noise density

Now take \(\lambda\) to be Lebesgue measure on \(\D=\mathbb{R}^{N_d}\), and assume the noise law \(\mu_\eta\) has a Lebesgue density \(\rho_\eta(\xi)=d\mu_\eta/d\lambda(\xi)\). The choice of Lebesgue measure is not cosmetic: the computation below slides the density along a translation, and Lebesgue measure is translation-invariant. For a general reference measure \(\lambda\), the density of the translated noise law with respect to \(\lambda\) is not simply \(\rho_\eta(d-G(m))\) — one would pick up a Radon–Nikodým correction comparing \(\lambda\) to its own translate (and if \(\lambda\) and its translate are mutually singular, no such density exists at all).

Testing \(L(m,\cdot)\) against a bounded measurable function \(f\) and performing the change of variable \(d=G(m)+\xi\) (using translation invariance of Lebesgue measure):

\[\int_\D f(d)\,L(m,dd)=\int_\D f(d)\,\rho_\eta\!\bigl(d-G(m)\bigr)\,\lambda(dd).\]

By the Radon–Nikodým theorem, the likelihood is:

\[L(d\mid m)=\frac{dL(m,\cdot)}{d\lambda}(d)=\rho_\eta\!\bigl(d-G(m)\bigr).\]
Intuition

The likelihood is the noise density evaluated at the residual \(d-G(m)\) — the mismatch between the observed data and the forward-model prediction. Whether a small residual is favoured depends entirely on the shape and centring of the noise density. For the familiar centred, unimodal noise models — Gaussian noise \(\rho_\eta(r)\propto\exp(-\tfrac12\lVert r\rVert^2_{\mathbf{C}^{-1}})\) being the canonical example — the density decreases with the size of the residual, so small residuals do mean high likelihood. But an arbitrary noise density can be asymmetric, multimodal, or even vanish at zero (noise concentrated near \(\pm 1\) makes a perfect data fit unlikely!). The exact statement is: the likelihood equals the noise density at the residual — nothing more, nothing less.

The posterior for additive noise

Substituting the likelihood into Bayes' relation, the posterior measure is:

\[\mu_{\M\mid D=d}(dm)=\frac{\rho_\eta\!\bigl(d-G(m)\bigr)\,\muMzero(dm)}{Z(d)},\]

where the evidence is:

\[Z(d)=\int_{\M}\rho_\eta\!\bigl(d-G(m)\bigr)\,\muMzero(dm).\]

This is the measure-theoretic Bayes' formula for inverse problems with additive noise. It requires no division by point probabilities. The denominator \(Z(d)\) is an integral over the model space — it is finite and positive under standard assumptions, even when the data space is continuous and \(\muD(\{d\})=0\).

A finite-dimensional story — signpost for infinite dimensions

Everything in this additive-noise section leaned on Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}^{N_d}\). In infinite-dimensional data or model spaces there is no Lebesgue measure to lean on — no σ-finite, translation-invariant measure exists on an infinite-dimensional Banach space. Worse, translating a Gaussian measure can produce a mutually singular measure unless the shift lies in its Cameron–Martin space. The right infinite-dimensional formulation therefore expresses the posterior as a density with respect to the prior, \(\frac{d\mu^{d}}{d\mu^{0}}(m)\propto\exp[-\Phi(m;d)]\), rather than giving prior and posterior densities with respect to a (nonexistent) flat volume measure. This is the standard formulation of Bayesian inverse problems on function spaces; the finite-dimensional formulas of this section are its Lebesgue-dominated special case.

What we have achieved

Let us take stock. We started in finite land with a simple ratio formula. We then climbed to the measure-theoretic level:

  • Part 3c: Conditional expectation defines conditional probability via integrals, avoiding division by zero.
  • Part 3d: Disintegration bundles eventwise conditional probabilities into conditional probability measures.
  • Part 3e: Bayes' formula emerges as the equality of two disintegrations of the same joint law. The familiar density form is a special case.

The measure-theoretic view keeps the underlying objects visible:

\[\text{prior measure}, \qquad \text{observation kernel}, \qquad \text{joint measure}, \qquad \text{posterior kernel}.\]

This is the language needed for regular conditional probabilities and for Bayesian inverse problems on general spaces.

Warning

In continuous spaces, \(\muD(\{\dobs\})=0\). The density formula \(\pi(m\mid d)\) is not the foundation — it is a representation of the posterior-kernel construction relative to chosen reference measures. The kernel form works in both finite and continuous settings.

In Part 3f, we will take the integral Bayes formula we have just derived and apply it back to the finite switch problem — bringing everything home to where we started.
