Part 3c: Conditional Expectation

Conditioning on σ-algebras, conditional probability, and the resolution of the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox.

Part of Bayes, Measure-Theoretically.

1 The Game 2 High-School Solution 3a Points → Events 3b Conditioning 3c Cond. Expectation 3d Disintegration 3e Bayesian Inversion 3f Back to Finite 4 Summary
A word before we begin

The next few parts are the most mathematically demanding. We are going to introduce conditional expectation, disintegration, and Bayesian inversion in measure-theoretic language. These concepts take effort to absorb, but they will click into place — trust the process.

One stylistic choice: even though our switch problem lives in finite land, we will write definitions using integrals rather than sums. This is deliberate. Integrals are the more general notation: they work in both finite and continuous spaces, and they lead to cleaner derivations. If you want to stay in finite land, just mentally replace every integral by a weighted sum — everything will follow exactly the same.

The payoff for battling the infinite case now: by the end of Part 3f, we will take the full measure-theoretic Bayes formula and apply it back to the finite switch problem, bringing everything home to where we started.

Conditioning on a σ-algebra: the switch problem

In Part 3b, we saw that conditioning on individual events breaks down when the conditioning event has probability zero. The Borel–Kolmogorov paradox showed that different ways of “shrinking” toward a measure-zero set can give different answers.

The resolution is to condition on a σ-algebra rather than on a set. To understand what this means, we will use a coarser σ-algebra on the switch problem's model space.

A coarser σ-algebra on \(\M\)

Recall that the full σ-algebra on the model space is \(\SigmaM=2^{\M}\) — every subset is an event. But we can also work with a coarser σ-algebra, one that cannot distinguish between certain outcomes.

Define:

\[\mathcal{G}=\{\varnothing,\;\{00,01\},\;\{10,11\},\;\M\}.\]

This σ-algebra cannot distinguish between:

  • outcomes \(00\) and \(01\) (both in \(\{00,01\}\)),
  • outcomes \(10\) and \(11\) (both in \(\{10,11\}\)).

So \(\mathcal{G}\) represents the information: “I only know whether the first switch is off (\(00\) or \(01\)) or on (\(10\) or \(11\)).”

A σ-algebra is a collection of distinctions

A σ-algebra is not only a collection of events. It is a collection of distinctions we are allowed to make. \(\mathcal{G}\) can ask: “is the first switch off or on?” But it cannot ask: “is the state exactly \(00\)?” because \(\{00\}\notin\mathcal{G}\).

The test is simple: \(A\in\mathcal{G}\) if and only if the coarse observer can decide whether \(A\) happened. This will make \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurability feel natural later: a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function is one that never uses more information than \(\mathcal{G}\) allows.

Conditional expectation: averaging over indistinguishable outcomes

Before we condition on a σ-algebra, let us recall what the ordinary expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) does. It averages \(X\) over all outcomes, weighted by their probabilities. It is the answer to the question: If I know nothing, what is my best guess for \(X\)?

Let \(X:\M\to\mathbb{R}\) be a random variable on the model space. For concreteness, suppose:

\[X(00)=0,\quad X(01)=2,\quad X(10)=1,\quad X(11)=3.\]

The ordinary expectation is the prior-weighted average over all four outcomes:

\[\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{m\in\M}\muMzero(\{m\})\,X(m) =0.4\cdot 0+0.1\cdot 2+0.1\cdot 1+0.4\cdot 3=1.5.\]

This is a single number. It is the best guess for \(X\) when we have no information at all — when we cannot distinguish any outcome from any other. In σ-algebra language, this is conditioning on the coarsest possible σ-algebra \(\{\varnothing,\M\}\), which cannot distinguish anything. Ordinary expectation is conditional expectation with no information.

Expectation as compression

If you must replace \(X\) by one number, and you know nothing, then \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) is the value that preserves the average. It is a one-number compression of \(X\) — the best you can do when the only visible events are \(\{\varnothing,\M\}\).

Now suppose we have partial information. Specifically, suppose we know which group the outcome belongs to in the coarser σ-algebra \(\mathcal{G}=\{\varnothing,\{00,01\},\{10,11\},\M\}\), but we cannot distinguish outcomes within each group. What is our best guess for \(X\) now?

The answer is the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\). It is no longer a single number — it is a random variable, because the best guess depends on which group we find ourselves in. Ordinary expectation compressed \(X\) to one number because we could not distinguish anything. Conditional expectation compresses \(X\) to one value per visible information cell, because we can distinguish groups but not individuals within them.

Two design rules

We want a replacement \(Y\) for \(X\) that satisfies two rules:

1. \(Y\) must be \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable. It can only use the information visible to \(\mathcal{G}\). It cannot secretly distinguish \(00\) from \(01\) if \(\mathcal{G}\) cannot.

2. \(Y\) must preserve all visible totals. On every event the coarse observer can actually see, the total weighted value of \(Y\) must equal that of \(X\).

Conditional expectation is the visible replacement of \(X\) that preserves all visible totals.

Formally: the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is the (essentially unique) \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable \(Y\) such that:

\[\int_H Y\,d\muMzero = \int_H X\,d\muMzero \qquad\text{for every } H\in\mathcal{G}.\]

If you just felt a slight panic at the sight of an integral — don't worry. In our finite setting, an integral against a measure is simply a weighted sum. The condition above just says:

\[\sum_{m\in H} Y(m)\,\muMzero(\{m\}) = \sum_{m\in H} X(m)\,\muMzero(\{m\}) \qquad\text{for every } H\in\mathcal{G}.\]

That is: the total weighted value of \(Y\) on each group must equal the total weighted value of \(X\) on that same group. We write it as an integral because the same definition works in continuous spaces, where sums become integrals — but the idea is identical: match the totals on each indistinguishable group.

This is a statement about integrals, not about ratios. There is no normalization, no division by \(\muMzero(H)\). The conditional expectation is the \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function whose integrals over \(\mathcal{G}\)-sets match those of \(X\). Existence and uniqueness (up to null sets) follow from the Radon–Nikodým theorem.

Why no normalization?

If the definition involved dividing by \(\muMzero(H)\), we would be back to the same problem: in continuous spaces, \(\muMzero(H)\) can be zero. The integral condition avoids this entirely. It only requires that the total mass of \(Y\) and \(X\) agree on each \(\mathcal{G}\)-set — no division anywhere.

In our finite example, this integral condition is easy to solve. Because \(Y\) must be \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, it must be constant on each group. Why? Recall that \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable means that for every interval \(I\subset\mathbb{R}\), the set of outcomes where \(Y\in I\) must belong to \(\mathcal{G}\). In our case, \(\mathcal{G}=\{\varnothing,\{00,01\},\{10,11\},\M\}\). If \(Y\) took different values on \(00\) and \(01\) — say \(Y(00)=a\) and \(Y(01)=b\) with \(a\neq b\) — then the preimage of a small interval around \(a\) would contain \(00\) but not \(01\), i.e. \(\{00\}\). But \(\{00\}\notin\mathcal{G}\). So \(Y\) cannot distinguish \(00\) from \(01\); it must assign them the same value. The same argument applies to \(\{10,11\}\). In general, a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function is constant on the atoms (finest indivisible sets) of \(\mathcal{G}\).

On \(\{00,01\}\), the condition \(\int_{\{00,01\}} Y\,d\muMzero = \int_{\{00,01\}} X\,d\muMzero\) becomes:

\[Y\cdot\muMzero(\{00,01\}) = \muMzero(\{00\})\cdot X(00)+\muMzero(\{01\})\cdot X(01).\]

Since \(\muMzero(\{00,01\})=0.5>0\), we can divide here (it is a consequence of the definition, not the definition itself):

\[Y\big|_{\{00,01\}}=\frac{0.4\cdot 0+0.1\cdot 2}{0.5}=0.4.\]

Similarly, on \(\{10,11\}\):

\[Y\big|_{\{10,11\}}=\frac{0.1\cdot 1+0.4\cdot 3}{0.5}=2.6.\]

Therefore:

\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}](m)=\begin{cases}0.4,&m\in\{00,01\},\\ 2.6,&m\in\{10,11\}.\end{cases}\]
But what if the group has measure zero?

A sharp-eyed reader will ask: “You divided by \(\muMzero(\{00,01\})\). What if that were zero? Aren't you back to dividing by nothing?”

No — and this is the beauty of the definition. The integral equality is meant to stay an equality. If \(\muMzero(H)=0\) for some \(H\in\mathcal{G}\), then every subset of \(H\) also has measure zero, so the right-hand side \(\sum_{m\in H} X(m)\,\muMzero(\{m\})\) is zero too. The equation reads \(0=0\), which is trivially true for any value of \(Y\) on that group. The conditional expectation is simply not determined on null sets — and it doesn't need to be, because those outcomes never occur.

The definition sidesteps division by zero entirely, like a spy ducking under laser beams. The integral condition never asks us to divide; it only asks us to match totals. When the total is zero, there is nothing to match.

Notice the progression: \(\mathbb{E}[X]=1.5\) is a single number (no information), while \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is a random variable that takes different values on different groups (partial information). If we conditioned on the full σ-algebra \(\SigmaM\), we would recover \(X\) itself (complete information).

Intuition

The ordinary expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) averages over all outcomes — the best guess when you know nothing. The conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is defined by an integral condition: it is the \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function whose integrals over \(\mathcal{G}\)-sets match those of \(X\). In the discrete case this simplifies to the familiar average within each group, but the definition itself involves no normalization — and that is precisely why it continues to work in continuous spaces where division would fail.

From conditional expectation to conditional probability

So far \(X\) could be any numerical quantity. But we are interested in probabilities, not arbitrary averages. The bridge is simple: probability is already an expectation in disguise.

Given an event \(A\in\SigmaM\), its indicator function \(\mathbf{1}_A:\M\to\{0,1\}\) is a random variable: it equals \(1\) on \(A\) and \(0\) outside. An indicator is a question turned into a random variable: it asks “did \(A\) happen?” and outputs yes (1) or no (0). The expectation of this indicator is just the probability of \(A\):

\[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{P}(A).\]

So if expectation of an indicator gives probability, then conditional expectation of an indicator should give conditional probability. We apply the machine to yes/no variables: condition on \(\mathcal{G}\). By the same integral definition, the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is the \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable satisfying:

\[\int_H \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A\mid\mathcal{G}]\,d\muMzero = \int_H \mathbf{1}_A\,d\muMzero = \muMzero(A\cap H) \qquad\text{for every } H\in\mathcal{G}.\]

We define the conditional probability of \(A\) given \(\mathcal{G}\) by:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G}):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A\mid\mathcal{G}].\]

This is the measure-theoretic definition of conditional probability. It is defined by the integral condition \(\int_H \mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})\,d\muMzero = \muMzero(A\cap H)\), not by dividing by \(\muMzero(H)\). No limiting procedure, no normalization. It is simply conditional expectation applied to an indicator function.

Concretely, in our example, let \(A=\{00,11\}\) (the event that both switches agree). Then \(\mathbf{1}_A(00)=1\), \(\mathbf{1}_A(01)=0\), \(\mathbf{1}_A(10)=0\), \(\mathbf{1}_A(11)=1\). On the group \(\{00,01\}\), the integral condition gives:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})\big|_{\{00,01\}}\cdot\muMzero(\{00,01\}) =\muMzero(A\cap\{00,01\})=\muMzero(\{00\})=0.4,\]

so (since \(\muMzero(\{00,01\})=0.5>0\)):

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})\big|_{\{00,01\}} =\frac{0.4}{0.5}=0.8.\]

On the group \(\{10,11\}\):

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})\big|_{\{10,11\}}\cdot\muMzero(\{10,11\}) =\muMzero(A\cap\{10,11\})=\muMzero(\{11\})=0.4,\]

so:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})\big|_{\{10,11\}} =\frac{0.4}{0.5}=0.8.\]

So \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})=0.8\) everywhere — knowing the first switch does not change the probability that both switches agree. The division by \(0.5\) here is a consequence of the integral definition in the discrete case, not the definition itself. In continuous spaces, where the group probability may be zero, the integral condition still works — no division is needed.

Key idea

Conditional probability is conditional expectation of an indicator function: \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A\mid\mathcal{G}]\), defined by the integral condition \(\int_H \mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})\,d\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}(A\cap H)\). This is the bridge from the abstract notion of conditioning on a σ-algebra to the concrete probabilities we need for Bayesian inference. It works in discrete and continuous spaces alike, because it is built on integrals, not division.

From σ-algebras to conditioning on a random variable

So far we have conditioned on an abstract σ-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\). But in probability and Bayesian inference, information usually arrives through a random variable. In our switch problem, the observation is the reporter's output:

\[D:\M\to\D=\{0,1\}.\]

If we observe \(D\), what information have we actually learned? We have not learned the full model state \(m\). We have only learned the value \(D(m)\). Therefore, after observing \(D\), two model states \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are indistinguishable whenever

\[D(m_1)=D(m_2).\]

So the information contained in \(D\) is exactly the information that can separate model states according to their data value, but no further.

To turn this into a σ-algebra, take any data event \(B\subseteq\D\). For example, \(B=\{1\}\) asks: did the reporter output 1? This is a question in data space. To make it an event in model space, we pull it back through \(D\):

\[D^{-1}(B)=\{m\in\M:D(m)\in B\}.\]

This is the event: the model state is one whose generated data lies in \(B\). Preimages are the translation device from questions about data to events in the original probability space.

The σ-algebra generated by \(D\) is the collection of all such pullback events:

\[\sigma(D)=\{D^{-1}(B):B\in\SigmaD\}.\]

In words: \(\sigma(D)\) is the set of all events whose truth can be decided by looking only at \(D\). This σ-algebra partitions \(\M\) into the sets where \(D=0\) and where \(D=1\) — the “indistinguishable classes” given the report.

So conditioning on the random variable \(D\) means conditioning on the information it reveals:

\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid D]:=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\sigma(D)].\]

The same applies to probabilities. For an event \(A\), we define

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid D):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A\mid\sigma(D)].\]

This object is \(\sigma(D)\)-measurable, so it is only allowed to depend on the outcome through the observed value of \(D\). If two model states produce the same data, then \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid D)\) must assign them the same value. Otherwise it would be using information that \(D\) does not contain. In other words, \(\sigma(D)\)-measurable means: cannot distinguish points with the same \(D\)-value.

Therefore there exists a function \(k_A:\D\to[0,1]\) such that

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid D)=k_A(D).\]

This is the Doob–Dynkin lemma: any quantity measurable with respect to \(\sigma(D)\) can be written as a function of \(D\). In plain language: if a quantity only uses the information in \(D\), then it must be expressible as a function of the observed data value.

Once the observed data value is \(\dobs\), the conditional probability is obtained by evaluation:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid D=\dobs):=k_A(\dobs).\]

The important point is that the conditioning was not performed by dividing by \(\mathbb{P}(D=\dobs)\). The conditioning was performed at the level of the σ-algebra \(\sigma(D)\). The final evaluation at \(\dobs\) comes only after the conditional probability has already been constructed as a function of the data.

Key point

The conditioning is performed at the level of the σ-algebra \(\sigma(D)\). Evaluating at \(\dobs\) is merely the final step. This is why the measure-theoretic approach handles continuous data spaces: the conditional distribution is defined by disintegration, not by dividing by the probability of a point.

Conditioning on \(m_1=2\): BK ambiguity versus σ-algebra conditioning

We now illustrate the power of the σ-algebra approach with a concrete example on \(\mathbb{R}^2\). This example revisits the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox from Part 3b, but now we have the tools to resolve it.

Setup

Let \((M_1,M_2)\) be a random vector on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with a smooth joint density \(\rho(m_1,m_2)\) with respect to Lebesgue measure — the standard notion of “volume” in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), which assigns to each rectangle its area and extends to more general sets. Saying that \(\rho\) is a density with respect to Lebesgue measure simply means that probabilities are obtained by integrating \(\rho\) over sets: \(\mathbb{P}((M_1,M_2)\in A)=\int_A\rho\,dm_1\,dm_2\). Let \(A\subset\mathbb{R}^2\) be any measurable set. We wish to condition on \(m_1=2\). The set \(S:=\{(m_1,m_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2:m_1=2\}\) has probability zero, so naive conditioning on \(S\) is ill-defined.

The BK constructions revisited

In Part 3b, we saw two ways to “approach” the set \(S\) via limits. Let us now write down what those limits actually produce.

Vertical strips. Define \(S^{(v)}_\varepsilon:=(2-\varepsilon,\,2+\varepsilon)\times\mathbb{R}\) and the approximate conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}^{(v)}_\varepsilon(A):=\mathbb{P}(A\cap S^{(v)}_\varepsilon)/\mathbb{P}(S^{(v)}_\varepsilon)\). By the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, the limit exists and equals:

\[\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}^{(v)}_\varepsilon(A) =\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_A(2,m_2)\,\rho(2,m_2)\,dm_2}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(2,m_2)\,dm_2}.\]

This defines a probability measure on the line \(m_1=2\) with density proportional to \(\rho(2,m_2)\). The limit exists — but it already depends on a geometric choice: the use of vertical strips.

Tilted tubes. Now define \(S^{(t)}_\varepsilon:=\{(m_1,m_2):|m_1-2|<\varepsilon|m_2|\}\) and the corresponding approximate conditional probability. A direct asymptotic expansion shows:

\[\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}^{(t)}_\varepsilon(A) =\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|m_2|\,\mathbf{1}_A(2,m_2)\,\rho(2,m_2)\,dm_2}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|m_2|\,\rho(2,m_2)\,dm_2}.\]

This is a different probability measure on the same line \(m_1=2\), obtained by weighting the density with \(|m_2|\). Both limits condition on the same measure-zero set, yet they produce different conditional distributions. This is the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox: the answer depends on the geometry of the limiting procedure, which probability theory does not specify.

The resolution: conditioning via \(\sigma(M_1)\)

Now let us do it properly. Define the data random variable \(D:=M_1\). This induces the σ-algebra:

\[\sigma(D)=\sigma(M_1)=\{B\times\mathbb{R}:B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}.\]
What are Borel sets?

\(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is the collection of Borel sets — the sets you can build from intervals by taking countable unions, countable intersections, and complements. Think of them as “any set you might reasonably want to measure on the real line.” Intervals, open sets, closed sets, single points, and finite unions of intervals are all Borel sets. In practice, every set we encounter in probability is Borel, so you can read \(B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) as “any reasonable subset of \(\mathbb{R}\).”

This σ-algebra encodes exactly the information “the value of \(m_1\) is observed.” For any measurable set \(A\), the conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid\sigma(M_1))\) is defined via conditional expectation:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid\sigma(M_1)):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A\mid\sigma(M_1)].\]

By the Doob–Dynkin lemma, there exists a measurable function \(k_A:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\) such that \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid\sigma(M_1))(m_1,m_2)=k_A(m_1)\) almost everywhere. The defining identity of conditional expectation:

\[\int_{B\times\mathbb{R}} k_A(m_1)\,d\mathbb{P} =\mathbb{P}(A\cap(B\times\mathbb{R}))\qquad\forall\,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\]

uniquely determines \(k_A\) (a.e.). A direct computation yields:

\[k_A(m_1)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_A(m_1,m_2)\,\rho(m_1,m_2)\,dm_2}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(m_1,m_2)\,dm_2}\quad\text{for a.e. }m_1.\]

Evaluating at \(m_1=2\) gives:

\[\mathbb{P}(A\mid M_1=2)=k_A(2)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_A(2,m_2)\,\rho(2,m_2)\,dm_2}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(2,m_2)\,dm_2}.\]

This coincides with the vertical-strip limit — but now it is uniquely determined by the σ-algebra \(\sigma(M_1)\) and the joint distribution. No geometric choice was involved.

But didn't you choose the vertical strips?

A natural objection: “You ended up with the vertical-strip answer. So you secretly chose that geometry — you just wrapped it in fancy notation.”

Not quite. The vertical strips are not chosen because they look nice. They are chosen because they are the sets of indistinguishability of the observation \(D=M_1\). Two points \((m_1,m_2)\) and \((m_1',m_2')\) with \(m_1=m_1'\) produce the same observed value — the observation cannot tell them apart. The σ-algebra \(\sigma(M_1)\) is built from exactly these indistinguishable classes: vertical strips \(B\times\mathbb{R}\).

If the observation were different — say \(D=M_1\cdot M_2\) — the indistinguishable classes would be hyperbolic curves, not vertical strips. The σ-algebra \(\sigma(D)\) would reflect that geometry automatically. No manual choice is made; the observation mapping determines the σ-algebra, and the σ-algebra determines the conditioning.

In other words: the geometry is not chosen for aesthetic reasons — it is imposed by what the observation can and cannot distinguish. This is what makes σ-algebra conditioning canonical.

But what about the σ-algebra on the data space?

A deeper objection: “Fine — the σ-algebra \(\sigma(D)\) on the model space is determined by \(D\). But \(\sigma(D)\) is built from preimages of sets in the data space \(\D\). How do you choose the σ-algebra on \(\D\)? The final answer depends on that choice too! So your answer is still not canonical.”

This is a fair point, and the answer is that the σ-algebra on the data space does matter — but its choice is rooted in the physics of the problem and the coarseness of observations. Can the measurement distinguish two data values that differ by arbitrarily small difference? Then singletons are effectively distinguishable, and we use the finest σ-algebra (the Borel one). Or perhaps the instrument bins its output: all values in \([0,0.1)\) are reported as “0”, all in \([0.1,0.2)\) as “1”, and so on. Then the data σ-algebra is coarser — it contains the bins, not individual points — and the induced \(\sigma(D)\) on the model space reflects that coarseness.

In each case, the choice is defensible and canonical for the physical situation. It is not arbitrary — it encodes what the instrument can actually resolve. In practice, we most often assume that observations can be distinguished down to singletons (individual points in the data space), so we equip \(\D\) with the Borel σ-algebra — the finest one compatible with the topology of the data space. This is the standard convention, and it is the one we adopt throughout this essay.

To be explicit: we are dealing with two σ-algebras here. The first is the one we choose on the data space \(\D\), based on the physics of what the instrument can resolve. The second is \(\sigma(D)\) on the model space, which is automatically determined by the random variable \(D\) — we do not choose it, the observation mapping constructs it for us. And \(D\) itself is chosen by us based on physical arguments: it encodes how the model produces the data. So the chain is: physics determines the data σ-algebra and the observation mapping; the observation mapping determines \(\sigma(D)\); \(\sigma(D)\) determines the conditioning. Everything is rooted in the reality of the problem, and canonical with respect to that reality.

Key point

The two BK constructions differ because they introduce additional geometric structure (tube shapes) that is not specified by the observation model. Conditioning via the σ-algebra \(\sigma(M_1)\) is canonical: it is completely determined by what the data mapping \(D=M_1\) can distinguish and the joint distribution. BK ambiguity arises when conditioning on measure-zero sets via ad hoc geometric limits; conditioning via σ-algebras forbids such limits and yields a unique conditional distribution (almost everywhere).
