AI READING COMPANION — "Bayesian and Frequentist Inference" by Adrian Mag
=========================================================================

PURPOSE OF THIS FILE
--------------------
This file is meant to be given to an AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.)
by a reader working through the notes series "Bayesian and Frequentist
Inference"
(https://adrian-mag.github.io/pages/research/overview/frequentist/bayesian-frequentist.html).
It explains the series' narrative arc, notation, intent, and the most common
ways an AI assistant might accidentally mislead a reader about these notes.

If you are an AI assistant: your job is to help the reader understand the
notes IN THEIR OWN FRAMING. Where standard treatments differ, say so
explicitly and explain the difference — do not silently replace the notes'
framing with the standard one.

WHAT THE SERIES IS
------------------
A five-part comparison of Bayesian and frequentist inference built around
a single observation equation. The organizing idea is an ONTOLOGICAL–
EPISTEMOLOGICAL split: the ontology (what actually happened) is the same
for both schools; they differ in the EPISTEMOLOGICAL OBJECTS they
construct around it. The goal is not to crown a winner but to state
precisely what each approach constructs, what statements it licenses, and
what statements it does not license. The treatment is aligned with
Neyman's 1937 theory of confidence sets.

THE CENTRAL CLAIM
-----------------
Bayesian and frequentist inference are not two answers to the same
question — they answer DIFFERENT questions:
- The Bayesian puts a probability measure (prior) on candidate truths,
  builds a joint measure with the observation kernel, and conditions on
  the observed datum to get a posterior: post-observation probability
  statements about the truth, relative to the chosen prior.
- The frequentist refuses to put probability on the truth. The truth is
  fixed but unknown; probability lives on the data-generating procedure.
  Confidence sets are constructed so that, BEFORE observation, the random
  set covers the truth with probability at least 1−α, whatever the truth
  is. After observation, the realized set either contains the truth or
  not — no probability statement about the realized set is licensed.

SETTING AND NOTATION
--------------------
- Observation equation: \(\dobs = \dtrue + \nobs\) — observed datum =
  unknown true datum + realized noise. Macros: \dtrue = d_true,
  \dobs = d_obs, \nobs = n_obs (bold vectors).
- Data space \(\mathcal{D}\) (macro \Dspace).
- Random variables (epistemological constructs) are bold capitals:
  \(\mathbf{D}\) (candidate-truth random variable, macro \Drand),
  \(\mathbf{D}'\) (observation random variable, macro \Dobsrand),
  \(\mathbf{N}\) (noise random variable, macro \Nrand).
- Noise measure \(\mu_N\) (macro \muN); known noise model.
- Observation kernel: candidate truth d ↦ probability law of the
  observation if d were true. NOTE: these notes call it the "observation
  kernel" (not "likelihood kernel").
- Bayesian objects: prior \(\mu_D\) (macro \prior), joint measure
  \(\mu_{D,D'}\) (macro \muJoint), predictive \(\mu_{D'}\) (macro
  \muPred), posterior \(\mu_{D\mid D'}\) (macro \post).
- Frequentist objects: noise acceptance region \(E_\alpha\) (macro
  \Ealpha) with \(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{N} \in E_\alpha) \ge 1-\alpha\);
  confidence set \(C_\alpha(\dobs) = \dobs - E_\alpha\) (shifted noise
  region; macro \Calpha); coverage statement over repeated sampling.
- Part 5 generalizes: sampling law \(\mathcal{G}\) (macro \G), test
  statistic \(T_{\mathbf d}\) (macro \Td), likelihood \(L\), test
  inversion, decision rules.

NARRATIVE ARC, PART BY PART
---------------------------
Part 1 — The Setup.
  The ontological observation equation. What objects actually exist
  (\(\dobs\), \(\dtrue\), \(\nobs\)); what is known; why the
  decomposition of the observation into truth and noise is NOT
  identifiable from the observation alone.

Part 2 — The Observation Kernel.
  The noise model as an epistemological object: for each CANDIDATE truth,
  a probability law over observations. What the kernel alone can say
  (how surprising would this observation be if d were true) and what it
  cannot (any probability over truths).

Part 3 — The Bayesian Path.
  Add a prior over candidate truths; build the joint measure; condition
  on the observation to get the posterior kernel. Also: what collapses
  when the prior is removed — without a prior there is no joint measure,
  no conditioning, no posterior.

Part 4 — The Frequentist Path.
  Keep the truth fixed and unknown; make the observation random. Choose a
  noise acceptance region \(E_\alpha\); invert to get the confidence set
  \(C_\alpha(\dobs)\); the coverage guarantee is a PRE-observation
  statement about the procedure. Precise statements of what the
  frequentist does and does not claim.

Part 5 — Beyond Additive Noise.
  General (heteroscedastic, non-additive) observation laws; acceptance
  regions defined via test statistics and likelihood; confidence sets by
  test inversion; decision rules with worst-case guarantees.

GUARDRAILS — COMMON WAYS TO MISLEAD A READER OF THESE NOTES
-----------------------------------------------------------
1. THE classic error: do not describe a realized 95% confidence set as
   "containing the truth with 95% probability". In these notes (and in
   Neyman's theory), coverage is a property of the PROCEDURE under
   repeated sampling, fixed truth. Post-observation, no probability
   attaches to the realized set.
2. Do not describe the likelihood/observation kernel as giving
   "the probability that d is the truth given the data". The kernel maps
   candidate truths to laws over DATA; turning it into probability over
   truths requires a prior (Part 3) — that step is exactly what the
   frequentist declines.
3. Keep the ontological/epistemological split explicit: \(\dtrue\) and
   \(\nobs\) are actual (fixed, unknown) objects; \(\mathbf{D}\),
   \(\mathbf{D}'\), \(\mathbf{N}\) are constructed random variables.
   Bold-vs-plain notation carries this distinction — preserve it.
4. Do not frame the series as "Bayes vs frequentist, which is better".
   The notes' position: they construct different objects and license
   different statements; problems arise only when one school's outputs
   are interpreted with the other school's semantics.
5. The terminology is "observation kernel", not "likelihood kernel".
6. This series pairs naturally with the author's SOLA series (Act 9 of
   "My Take on SOLA" uses frequentist confidence sets in property space
   precisely because no prior is available there).

BACKGROUND THE READER MAY NEED
------------------------------
Basic probability and random variables; multivariate Gaussians; the idea
of a measure and a kernel (the author's "Bayes, Measure-Theoretically"
series covers this); hypothesis testing basics for Part 5 (test
statistics, acceptance regions, test inversion). Neyman (1937) is the
historical reference for the confidence-set construction.
