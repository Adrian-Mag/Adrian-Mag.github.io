Bayesian and Frequentist Inference

From an ontological–epistemological split.

1 The Setup 2 The Observation Kernel 3 Bayesian Path 4 Frequentist Path 5 Beyond Additive

What to expect

Bayesian and frequentist inference do not merely give different answers to the same probabilistic question. They construct different epistemological objects around the same ontological situation.

The divide is old, and its founding documents are all linked from these pages: Bayes’s essay [Bay63] and Jeffreys’s Theory of Probability [Jef39] on one side; Fisher’s foundations [Fis22], the Neyman–Pearson theory of tests [NP33], and Neyman’s confidence estimation [Ney37] on the other. Efron’s “Why Isn’t Everyone a Bayesian?” [Efr86] is the classic short meditation on why both survive. Each part closes with the references behind its claims.

We begin with a simple observation equation: \[ \dobs=\dtrue+\nobs. \] Here \(\dobs\) is the observed datum, \(\dtrue\) is the unknown true datum, and \(\nobs\) is the realized noise. This equation is not yet Bayesian or frequentist — it is simply a statement about the relation between truth, observation, and error.

The Bayesian introduces a probability measure on possible true data values to represent uncertainty about \(\dtrue\), then conditions on \(\dobs\) to obtain posterior probabilities over possible truths. The frequentist refuses to assign a probability law to \(\dtrue\), keeps it fixed but unknown, places probability on the data-generating procedure, and constructs calibrated procedures with controlled repeated-sampling behaviour.

The goal is not to declare one perspective correct and the other incorrect. The goal is to make precise what each perspective is doing, what objects it constructs, what statements it licenses, and what statements it does not license.

The five parts

Part 1 — The Setup. The ontological observation equation: actual objects, what is known and what is hidden, and why the decomposition of \(\dobs\) into truth and noise is not identifiable from the observation alone.

Part 2 — The Observation Kernel. The noise model as an epistemological object. Candidate-dependent observation laws, the observation kernel as a map from candidate truths to probability measures on observations, and what it can and cannot say by itself.

Part 3 — The Bayesian Path. Introducing a prior, the epistemological true-datum random variable, the joint measure, the posterior kernel, and what collapses when the prior is removed.

Part 4 — The Frequentist Path. Truth fixed, observation random. Noise acceptance regions, confidence sets from shifted noise, the coverage statement, and what the frequentist does and does not claim.

Part 5 — Beyond Additive Noise. Heteroscedastic and non-additive observation laws, the general likelihood-based construction of confidence sets, test statistics, and decision rules with worst-case guarantees.
