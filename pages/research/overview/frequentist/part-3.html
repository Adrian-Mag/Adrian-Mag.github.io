Part 3: The Bayesian Path

Prior, joint measure, posterior — and what collapses without one.

Part of Bayesian and Frequentist Inference.

Introducing a prior on the truth

The observation kernel gives, for each candidate true datum \(\mathbf d\), a probability law for possible observations. It has the direction \(\mathbf d \longmapsto \mu_{\mathbf d}\) — from candidate truth to observation law. This is not yet a probability law on possible true data values.

The Bayesian construction adds precisely this missing ingredient: a probability measure \(\prior\) on the true-data space \((\Dspace, \Sigma_{\Dspace})\), or equivalently a random variable \(\Drand \sim \prior\). This random variable is an epistemological representation of uncertainty about the unknown true datum. It is not introduced because reality itself contains a randomly fluctuating true datum, but because, from the perspective of the observer, the true datum is unknown.

Two objects, kept separate

Ontological true datum: \(\dtrue\) is the actual true datum. It is fixed, but generally hidden.

Epistemological true datum: \(\Drand \sim \prior\) is a random variable representing uncertainty about which value \(\dtrue\) might have.

The Bayesian observation model

Assuming \(\Drand\) and \(\Nrand\) are independent, the Bayesian observation model is \[ \Dobsrand = \Drand + \Nrand. \] This assumes that the data space \(\Dspace\) carries enough structure for addition to be meaningful — for instance, \(\Dspace=\mathbb{R}^n\) or a Banach space. This differs from the known-truth model \(\Dobsrand = \dtrue + \Nrand\) in that the centre of the observation law is itself epistemologically random: \(\Drand \sim \prior\). The Bayesian observation variable mixes two sources of uncertainty: prior uncertainty about the true datum (\(\Drand\)) and noise uncertainty (\(\Nrand\)).

The prior predictive distribution

The law of \(\Dobsrand\) is called the prior predictive distribution. It describes what observations are expected before the actual datum \(\dobs\) is used: \[ \muPred(A) = \int_{\Dspace} \mu_{\mathbf d}(A) \,\prior(d\mathbf d). \] In the additive independent case, this is the convolution \(\muPred = \prior * \muN\). If densities exist: \[ p_{\muPred}(\mathbf d') = \int_{\Dspace} p_N(\mathbf d' - \mathbf d)\, p_{\prior}(\mathbf d)\,d\mathbf d. \]

The joint measure

The Bayesian construction defines a joint probability measure on the pair \((\Drand, \Dobsrand)\): \[ \muJoint(B \times A) = \int_B \mu_{\mathbf d}(A)\,\prior(d\mathbf d). \] This joint measure contains the main Bayesian objects:

  • Prior: the marginal law of \(\Drand\).
  • Prior predictive: the marginal law of \(\Dobsrand\).
  • Observation kernel: the conditional law of \(\Dobsrand\) given \(\Drand = \mathbf d\).
  • Posterior kernel: the conditional law of \(\Drand\) given \(\Dobsrand = \mathbf d'\).

The likelihood appears inside the Bayesian construction as the conditional law of the observation given the epistemological true datum: \(\L(\mathbf d, A) = \Prob(\Dobsrand \in A \mid \Drand = \mathbf d)\). What is distinctively Bayesian is the multiplication of this sampling model by a prior measure on possible true data values.

The posterior kernel

The posterior kernel reverses the direction of the observation kernel. The observation kernel goes from candidate truth to observation law; the posterior goes from observed datum to probability measure on possible truths: \[ \post(\mathbf d', B) = \Prob(\Drand \in B \mid \Dobsrand = \mathbf d'). \] When \(\Dobsrand\) is continuous, the event \(\{\Dobsrand = \mathbf d'\}\) has probability zero, so the posterior kernel should be understood as a regular conditional distribution — a version of the conditional law defined \(\muPred\)-almost everywhere, which exists under standard conditions (e.g. standard Borel or Radon spaces). After the actual observation \(\dobs\), the Bayesian posterior is \(\post(\dobs, \cdot)\), evaluated using a regular version well-behaved at \(\dobs\). If densities exist, Bayes' formula gives \[ p(\mathbf d \mid \dobs) = \frac{p_N(\dobs - \mathbf d)\,p_{\prior}(\mathbf d)} {p_{\muPred}(\dobs)}, \] or equivalently \(p(\mathbf d \mid \dobs) \propto p_N(\dobs - \mathbf d)\,p_{\prior}(\mathbf d)\).

The posterior combines two ingredients: data fit \(p_N(\dobs - \mathbf d)\), which measures how well candidate \(\mathbf d\) explains the observed datum, and prior weight \(p_{\prior}(\mathbf d)\), which measures how plausible \(\mathbf d\) was before observing.

The point of contact with reality

The actual observation \(\dobs\) is an ontological object. The Bayesian model contains an epistemological random variable \(\Dobsrand\) representing possible observations before the actual observation is used. The point of contact is the identification \(\Dobsrand = \dobs\). Conditioning on this event injects information from reality into the epistemological model.

The Bayesian workflow: \[ \prior \quad\longrightarrow\quad \muJoint \quad\longrightarrow\quad \post(\dobs, \cdot). \] Before observation, uncertainty is represented by the prior. After observation, uncertainty is represented by the posterior. This is why the Bayesian can make statements such as \(\Prob(\Drand \in B \mid \Dobsrand = \dobs) = \post(\dobs, B)\) — these are posterior probability statements about the epistemological true-datum random variable, not statements about repeated confidence-set coverage.

Bayesian outlier assessment

The Bayesian can ask whether \(\dobs\) is surprising before conditioning, using the prior predictive distribution \(\muPred = \prior * \muN\). A small value of \(p_{\muPred}(\dobs)\) indicates that the observed datum lies in a low-density region of the prior predictive distribution.

However, a prior predictive surprise can occur for several reasons: the observation may require an extreme noise realization around a plausible true datum (noise outlier), the true datum may lie in a region of low prior probability (prior surprise), or the model may be misspecified. After observing \(\dobs\), the posterior can help distinguish which explanation is favoured.

Bayesian advantage and price

The advantage: Bayes can make posterior probability statements about possible truths and explanations. The price: these statements depend on the prior and the full probabilistic model.

What collapses without a prior

Without the prior \(\prior\), several Bayesian objects cannot be constructed:

  • No epistemological true-datum random variable \(\Drand \sim \prior\).
  • No Bayesian predictive observation variable \(\Dobsrand = \Drand + \Nrand\).
  • No prior predictive distribution \(\muPred\) — there is no measure with which to average the candidate-dependent observation laws.
  • No joint measure on \((\Drand, \Dobsrand)\).
  • No posterior kernel \(\post(\mathbf d', B)\) — there is nothing to condition.

The prior is not a decorative extra. It is the object that makes posterior probability over possible truths mathematically meaningful.

What survives without a prior

Although the full Bayesian construction collapses, not everything disappears. The noise model \(\Nrand \sim \muN\) survives. The candidate-dependent observation laws \(\mu_{\mathbf d} = (T_{\mathbf d})_{\#}\muN\) survive. The observation kernel \(\L(\mathbf d, A) = \mu_{\mathbf d}(A)\) survives. The ability to test whether \(\dobs - \mathbf d\) is plausible under the noise model for each candidate \(\mathbf d\) survives.

The dividing line

Survives without a prior: candidate-wise sampling laws.

Collapses without a prior: probability over candidate truths.

Without a prior, one does not lose the observation model. One loses the ability to turn the observation model into posterior probabilities over possible truths. This surviving sampling structure is the starting point for frequentist inference.

Why posterior probabilities are unavailable

The posterior probability statement \(\Prob(\dtrue \in A \mid \dobs) = 0.95\) is not automatically meaningful. The difficulty is that \(\dtrue\) is an ontological object, not a random variable. For the expression to be a probability statement, one must first introduce a probability model in which the true datum is represented by a random variable — exactly what the Bayesian does with \(\Drand \sim \prior\).

The likelihood alone cannot fix this. The likelihood function \(\ell_{\dobs}(\mathbf d) = p_N(\dobs - \mathbf d)\) is a function of the candidate true datum, but it is not automatically a probability density over \(\mathbf d\). It may or may not be normalizable with respect to a chosen reference measure on \(\Dspace\), but that reference measure is extra structure — it is not supplied by the observation kernel alone. Even when normalization happens to work (as in the additive location model with Lebesgue measure), treating the likelihood as a density over \(\mathbf d\) corresponds morally to using a flat or improper prior, a construction that is not invariant under reparameterization. The likelihood answers: how plausible is the observation if \(\mathbf d\) were true? The posterior answers: how plausible is \(\mathbf d\) after observing \(\dobs\)? The second question cannot be obtained from the first without additional structure.

Without a prior, one may say “candidate \(\mathbf d\) is compatible or incompatible with \(\dobs\) under the noise model,” but not “the probability that \(\dtrue\) lies in \(A\) given \(\dobs\) is \(0.95\).” That latter statement belongs to a Bayesian construction, or to another framework that introduces a post-data probability measure on possible true values.
