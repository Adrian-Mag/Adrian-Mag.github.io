The Box Everyone Knows

Movement I · What you are actually talking to

Act 1 Current as of — (set at publication)

You have used one. A box, a cursor, an answer. Perhaps it was useful. Perhaps it told you something false with complete confidence, and you have not been back since. Both of those things happened, and neither of them is evidence of what you probably concluded from it.

The interface is the same everywhere. One box. You type into it, an answer appears, and that is the whole of the machine as far as anyone can see. It is a remarkably honest interface in one respect: it hides nothing on purpose. It is also, for the same reason, deeply misleading — because there is no visible difference between the box that has almost nothing behind it and the box that has a great deal.

Most people’s judgement of this technology was formed at that box. That is a reasonable place to form it. It is also, I want to argue, a bit like judging an engine by driving a car around a car park: everything you observed was real, and almost none of it was about the engine.

Two bad experiences that look identical

Think about the last time one of these systems disappointed you. It was probably one of two failures, and they have almost nothing in common.

The first is the famous one. You asked for a fact and got a confident fabrication — a citation that does not exist, a function that was never in the library, a date off by three years. This is the failure everyone can name, and it is the one that has genuinely receded. Not vanished. Receded.

The second is quieter and, I think, far more common now. You asked something reasonable and got an answer that was not wrong, exactly, but was useless. Generic. Correct for someone, somewhere, in some situation — just not for you, in yours. You wanted advice about your codebase, and got advice about codebases. You wanted to know what a term means in your field, and got the meaning it has in someone else’s.

The first failure is a defect in the model. The second is not a defect at all. It is the entirely correct behaviour of a system that was never told which situation it was in — and had no way to find out.

The question this series is actually about

If you conclude from that second experience that the technology is oversold, you have drawn a conclusion about capability from an observation about context. The model answered the question it was given. The question it was given was missing almost everything that would have made a good answer possible: what you are working on, what you already tried, what your constraints are, what is in the file you are staring at, what your field means by that word.

None of that arrived, because nothing was there to bring it. The box does not know about your files. It cannot look anything up unless someone built it a way to look things up. It has no memory of yesterday unless someone gave it one. Everything a capable assistant would need to know, it does not have — and the interface gives you no hint that this is what is missing.

So the interesting question is not how smart is the model? That question has a moving answer and everyone is already shouting about it. The interesting question is: what would you have to build around it before its answers were about your situation rather than a generic one?

That thing has a name. It is called a harness, and almost everything that changed in the past few years happened in it rather than in the model. People who get startling results are not, for the most part, using a different model from you. They are using a different machine around it.

To see why that machine is necessary — and why it takes the peculiar shape it does — it helps to know exactly what sits at the centre of it. That is the next act, and it is shorter than you might fear.
