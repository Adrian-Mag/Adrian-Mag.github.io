Inside the Kernel

Movement I · What you are actually talking to

Act 2 Current as of July 2026

Strip away the interface, the memory, the tools, the safety systems, and the personality, and what remains is smaller and stranger than the word “intelligence” suggests. It is a function. Text goes in; a probability distribution over the next chunk of text comes out. That is the whole of it.

We are going to spend one act on the model itself and then leave it alone. Not because it is uninteresting. The inside of these things is one of the more remarkable objects of the last decade. But almost nothing in the rest of this series depends on it. You need a working picture, not a full one, and the working picture is simple.

It does not read words

Before anything else happens, your text is chopped into tokens: fragments, roughly sub-word sized, drawn from a fixed vocabulary decided before training. Common words usually survive intact. Unusual ones shatter. A leading space is generally part of the token rather than a separator, so ·the and the are different tokens entirely.

This sounds like trivia. It is not: it is the unit in which everything is counted, billed, and limited. Every context window, every price, every truncation you will meet later in this series is measured in these things. Type something into the panel, ideally something from your own field, and watch where it breaks.

It does not produce an answer

Given a sequence of tokens, the model returns a probability distribution over what the next one might be. Not a decision. A distribution. Every token in its vocabulary receives some share, most of them vanishingly small.

Something outside the model then picks one. That choice is a policy, not a fact: take the most likely token every time and you get flat, repetitive text; sample from further down the tail and you get variety and more mistakes. The dial governing that trade is temperature, and it lives in the software around the model, not in the model.

Then the chosen token is appended to the input, and the whole thing runs again. And again. That loop (predict, pick, append, repeat) is how a paragraph gets written, one fragment at a time, with no plan for the sentence it is halfway through.

Notice what the second and third prompts do that the first does not. “The capital of France is” has one overwhelming continuation; the distribution barely has a tail. “My favourite colour is” spreads across several perfectly good answers, because there is no fact of the matter. So the same question, asked twice, can honestly give you two different replies. Much of what people experience as unreliability is this, seen from the outside.

Two different things called “layers”

Here is the confusion I most want to defuse, because it makes the rest of this series either obvious or impenetrable depending on which way it falls.

Inside the model there are layers: dozens of stacked arithmetic operations that the input passes through, each refining a numerical representation of the text. They were shaped during training, they are frozen afterwards, and you cannot change them. When someone mentions a “96-layer model”, this is what they mean.

Outside the model there are also layers: the system prompt, the conversation being replayed, retrieved documents, tool definitions, memory files, permission checks. These were written by engineers, and they change weekly. And here is the part that matters: you can change them too.

The two have nothing to do with each other beyond the shared word. This series is about the second kind, exclusively. If you have been carrying a vague sense that understanding the first kind is a prerequisite for understanding the second: it is not, and you can set it down.

What the model does not have

Worth stating flatly, because every later act is a consequence of one of these.

It has no memory. Two calls are unrelated events; the second does not know the first happened. It has no access to your files, or to the internet, or to anything else. It emits text and nothing more. It has no clock: it cannot tell whether a second or a year passed between calls, or what today’s date is. And it cannot act. It cannot run a command, send a message, or edit a file. It can produce text that describes doing so, and something else may choose to act on that text, but that something else is not the model.

Every one of those absences is filled, later, by machinery. That machinery is the subject of this series.

One thing that did happen inside

A raw predictor trained on text is not helpful. Continue “How do I sort a list in Python?” by pure prediction and a plausible continuation is three more forum questions, because that is what such text looks like in the wild.

Turning a predictor into something that answers rather than continues took additional training: demonstrations of desired behaviour, then tuning against human comparisons of competing outputs.1 That work is genuinely inside the model, and it is why the box responds to a question instead of writing more questions. It is also, more or less, where the inside stops mattering for us.

So: a function, with no memory, no senses, no hands, and no clock, that returns a distribution over text fragments. Everything you have seen it do beyond that was something else, wrapped around it: remembering your name, reading your file, searching the web, running your code.

The next act starts with the smallest such wrapper, the one you have already used.
