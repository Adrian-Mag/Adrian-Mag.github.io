Act V: The Data Space and the Adjoint

The adjoint is defined by inner products, not by a matrix transpose.

The Data Space

Finite-dimensional data

In the toy inverse problem, the data consist of finitely many real numbers: \[ \obs = \begin{pmatrix} [\obs]_1 \\ \vdots \\ [\obs]_K \end{pmatrix}. \] Thus, the natural data space is \(\D = \R^K\).

This is already a major simplification. The model may live in an infinite-dimensional function space, but the observations live in a finite-dimensional vector space. The forward map therefore compresses a model into finitely many numbers: \(G : \M \to \R^K\).

The data equation is \[ \obs = G m + \noise, \qquad \noise \sim \N(0,\Cd). \]

At first glance, finite-dimensional data seem completely harmless. But even here, a modelling choice remains hidden: we must decide how to measure lengths, angles, and residuals in the data space.

Intuition

The data space may be finite-dimensional, but it still has geometry. Ignoring that geometry is how a transpose sneaks into places where an adjoint was supposed to stand.

Why finite dimensions are easier

All norms induce the same topology

On \(\R^K\), many different norms can be defined. In finite dimensions, all norms induce the same topology. This means that they give the same notion of convergence. This is not true in infinite-dimensional spaces.

Remark

Although all norms on \(\R^K\) induce the same topology, they do not define the same geometry. They may measure length and angle differently. For inverse problems, this distinction matters.

Completeness is automatic

Every finite-dimensional normed vector space is complete. Therefore, data-space limits do not escape the space. This makes the data side of the inverse problem much easier than the model side.

Linear maps are continuous

Every linear map between finite-dimensional normed spaces is continuous. When the model space is infinite-dimensional, this automatic continuity is no longer guaranteed. A linear map \(G:\M\to\D\) must be checked.

Since \(\D=\R^K\) is finite-dimensional, checking continuity of \(G\) often reduces to checking the continuity of its individual components. For example, if \(G_i m = \int_\Omega K_i(x)\,m(x)\,dx\) and \(\M=L^2(\Omega)\), \(K_i\in L^2(\Omega)\), then by Cauchy-Schwarz, \(|G_i m| \leq \norm{K_i}_{L^2}\norm{m}_{L^2}\).

But geometry still matters

Choosing an inner product

A general inner product on \(\R^K\) can be written as \(\inner{a}{b}_{\D} = a^{\top} \Wb_D b\), where \(\Wb_D\in\R^{K\times K}\) is symmetric positive definite. If \(\Wb_D=\mathbf{I}_K\), this is the usual Euclidean inner product.

This choice affects the adjoint of the forward map. In particular, if the model space also has a non-Euclidean geometry, then the adjoint is not represented by the ordinary matrix transpose.

Warning

In finite dimensions, the transpose and the adjoint coincide only when the coordinate bases are orthonormal for the inner products being used.

Noise covariance and data weighting

The observational noise is modelled as \(\noise \sim \N(0,\Cd)\). The inverse covariance \(\Cd^{-1}\) is called the precision matrix. It weights the data residual \(r(m)=\obs-G m\). The usual Gaussian likelihood contains the quadratic form \(r(m)^{\top} \Cd^{-1} r(m)\).

A residual in a noisy datum is less serious than the same residual in a highly accurate datum.

Intuition

The covariance says how the noise wiggles. The precision says how strongly each residual is judged.

Whitened and unwhitened data

Sometimes it is convenient to transform the data so that the noise becomes standard Gaussian. Assume \(\Cd = L\mathbf{L}^{\top}\), where \(L\) is invertible. Define the whitened residual \(\widetilde{r}(m) = \mathbf{L}^{-1}(\obs-G m)\). Then \(\norm{\widetilde{r}(m)}_2^2 = r^{\top} \Cd^{-1} r\).

Whitening is often computationally useful, but it is not magic. It is a change of coordinates in data space. The underlying statistical model is the same.

The data misfit

The data misfit measures how well a model predicts the observed data. For Gaussian observational noise, the natural quadratic misfit is \(\norm{r(m)}_{\Cd^{-1}}^2\), and the likelihood is \(\pi(\obs\mid m) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\obs-G m)^{\top} \Cd^{-1}(\obs-G m) \right)\).

The Bayesian update is driven by the potential \(\Phi(m;\obs) = \frac{1}{2} \norm{\obs-G m}_{\Cd^{-1}}^2\).

Lesson

The data misfit is not merely a least-squares penalty. It is the negative log likelihood produced by the noise model.

The Forward Operator and Its Adjoint

The forward map

The forward map sends a model to predicted data: \(G : \M \to \D\). For the toy problem, \([G m]_i = \int_\Omega K_i(x)\,m(x)\,dx\).

If \(\M\) is a Hilbert space and \(\D\) is equipped with an inner product, then \(G\) has an adjoint, provided \(G\) is bounded. The adjoint tells us how data-space residuals are pulled back into model space.

The adjoint is not just a transpose

Definition of the adjoint

Let \(\M\) and \(\D\) be Hilbert spaces with inner products \(\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}_{\M}\) and \(\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}_{\D}\). The adjoint of a bounded linear operator \(G:\M\to\D\) is the unique bounded linear operator \(G^{\adj}:\D\to\M\) satisfying \[ \inner{G m}{d}_{\D} = \inner{m}{G^{\adj} d}_{\M} \] for all \(m\in\M\), \(d\in\D\).

This equation is the definition. It is not a computational trick. It says that the same pairing can be evaluated either by pushing the model forward into data space or by pulling the data vector back into model space.

Intuition

The forward map asks: "What data does this model predict?" The adjoint asks: "What model-space direction is suggested by this data-space quantity?"

Why inner products determine the adjoint

The adjoint depends on the inner products. Change the inner product, and the adjoint changes.

Suppose the data-space inner product is \(\inner{a}{b}_{\D}=a^{\top} \Wb_D b\), and the model-space inner product is \(\inner{u}{v}_{\M}=u^{\top} \Mb v\). Let the forward map be represented by a matrix \(\Gb\in\R^{K\times N}\). The adjoint matrix \(A\) must satisfy \(\inner{\Gb u}{d}_{\D} = \inner{u}{Ad}_{\M}\) for all \(u,d\).

The left-hand side is \((\Gb u)^{\T} \Wb_D d = u^{\top} \Gb^{\T} \Wb_D d\). The right-hand side is \(u^{\top} \Mb A d\). Therefore, \(\Mb A = \Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\), and \[ A = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D. \]

Only when \(\Mb=\mathbf{I}_N\) and \(\Wb_D=\mathbf{I}_K\) does this reduce to \(\Gb^{\T}\).

Matrix transpose versus Hilbert adjoint

The transpose is a coordinate operation. The Hilbert adjoint is a geometric operation. In Euclidean coordinates with orthonormal bases, these two operations coincide. That coincidence is useful, but it is also dangerous.

If the basis functions in the model space are not orthonormal, then the coefficient vectors do not inherit the Euclidean inner product. With basis functions \(\{\phi_j\}\), the inner product between two functions is \(\inner{m_N}{n_N}_{\M} = \mathbf{u}^{\top} \Mb\mathbf{v}\), not generally \(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\).

Warning

Using \(\Gb^{\T}\) as the adjoint when the basis is not orthonormal means silently using the wrong geometry.

The role of Gram matrices

Non-orthonormal bases

A basis \(\{\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N\}\) is orthonormal in \(\M\) if \(\inner{\phi_i}{\phi_j}_{\M} = \delta_{ij}\). Most finite element bases are not orthonormal. Hat functions overlap with neighbouring hat functions, so their inner products are not zero for neighbouring indices.

The model-space Gram matrix

The model-space Gram matrix is defined by \([\Mb]_{ij} = \inner{\phi_i}{\phi_j}_{\M}\). For \(\M=L^2(\Omega)\), this is the familiar finite element mass matrix: \([\Mb]_{ij} = \int_\Omega \phi_i(x)\phi_j(x)\,dx\).

For \(\M=H^1(\Omega)\), the Gram matrix becomes \([\Mb]_{ij} = \int_\Omega \phi_i(x)\phi_j(x)\,dx + \int_\Omega \phi_i'(x)\phi_j'(x)\,dx\). Thus, the same basis functions produce different Gram matrices depending on the chosen model-space geometry.

Intuition

The Gram matrix is the memory of the function-space geometry after we choose coordinates.

The data-space Gram matrix

Similarly, the data-space inner product may be written as \(\inner{a}{b}_{\D} = a^{\top} \Wb_D b\). The matrix \(\Wb_D\) is the Gram matrix of the data-space coordinate basis. In many finite-dimensional data problems, we choose Euclidean data coordinates and therefore take \(\Wb_D=\mathbf{I}_K\). But this is still a choice, not an inevitability.

The discrete adjoint

The discrete adjoint is the matrix \[ G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D. \] This formula is one of the simplest places where the naive discretization betrays itself. If we use \(\Gb^{\T}\) instead of \(\Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\), then we are not using the adjoint corresponding to the chosen model and data spaces.

Proposition

Let \(\Gb:\R^N\to\R^K\) be represented by a matrix \(\Gb\). Equip \(\R^N\) with the inner product \(\inner{u}{v}_{\M}=u^{\top} \Mb v\), where \(\Mb\) is symmetric positive definite, and equip \(\R^K\) with \(\inner{a}{b}_{\D}=a^{\top} \Wb_D b\), where \(\Wb_D\) is symmetric positive definite. Then the adjoint of \(\Gb\) with respect to these inner products is represented by \[ G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D. \]

The adjoint \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}}\) is defined by \(\inner{\Gb u}{d}_{\D} = \inner{u}{G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}}d}_{\M}\) for all \(u\in\R^N\) and \(d\in\R^K\). Expanding both inner products gives \(u^{\top} \Gb^{\T} \Wb_D d = u^{\top} \Mb G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} d\). Since this holds for all \(u\) and \(d\), we have \(\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D = \Mb G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}}\). Multiplying by \(\Mb^{-1}\) gives \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\).

The geometry-aware discretization

What changes

In the naive discretization, posterior formulas use \(\Gb^{\T}\). This is correct only when the relevant adjoint is represented by \(\Gb^{\T}\), which requires orthonormal coordinates. With non-orthonormal basis functions, the geometry-aware adjoint is \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\).

The posterior formulas must be interpreted as discretizations of the function-space formulas: \[ C^{\obs} = C_0 - C_0 G^{\adj} \left( G C_0 G^{\adj} + \Cd \right)^{-1} G C_0, \] \[ m^{\obs} = m_0 + C_0 G^{\adj} \left( G C_0 G^{\adj} + \Cd \right)^{-1} (\obs-G m_0). \] The object to discretize is \(G^{\adj}\), not the symbol \(\Gb^{\T}\).

What stays invariant

A geometry-aware discretization keeps the continuous problem fixed. Changing the basis should not change the mathematical problem. This is the point of including Gram matrices. They ensure that coordinate vectors remember the geometry of the functions they represent.

Intuition

The coordinates may change costume. The function-space problem should remain the same actor.

Why this prevents changing the problem by accident

When we use the wrong adjoint, we are not merely making a small algebraic mistake. We are changing the inverse problem. The adjoint appears in posterior mean formulas, posterior covariance formulas, gradient calculations, normal equations, data-to-model backprojection, and covariance updates.

If the adjoint is wrong, then residuals are pulled back into the wrong model-space directions. The posterior mean can shift. The uncertainty can be misrepresented. The discretized problem can stop approximating the intended continuous problem.

Lesson

The transpose belongs to Euclidean coordinates. The adjoint belongs to the spaces.

The moral of Act V

The data space was easy, but not empty of choices. Its finite-dimensional nature gave us automatic completeness and friendly convergence, but its inner product still mattered. The noise covariance told us how to weight residuals. Whitening showed that data weighting can be moved into coordinates, provided we do so consistently.

The forward map then forced us to confront the adjoint. The adjoint is defined by \(\inner{G m}{d}_{\D} = \inner{m}{G^{\adj} d}_{\M}\), which means it depends on the inner products of both spaces. Once basis functions enter, those inner products become Gram matrices. For a non-orthonormal model basis and a weighted data geometry, the discrete adjoint is \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\), not simply \(\Gb^{\T}\).

Checkpoint

We have now chosen the model space, the data space, the forward map, and the geometry needed to define the adjoint. The next act turns to the remaining ingredient: the prior measure. This is where the phrase "the prior is not just a matrix" finally gets its full teeth.
