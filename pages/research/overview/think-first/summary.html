Summary of the Argument

The central claim of this document is simple: a discretization should approximate an inverse problem. It should not define the inverse problem by accident.

The opening example showed how easily this accident can happen. We began with an unknown function, introduced a basis, placed a Gaussian prior directly on the coefficients, used an ordinary transpose as if it were automatically the adjoint, and computed a finite-dimensional posterior. The posterior mean looked reasonable. But posterior samples and mesh refinement revealed the problem: the prior did not correspond to a coherent probability measure on a function space.

The repair was not to find a cleverer diagonal matrix. The repair was to return to the modelling choices that should have been made before discretization.

The five modelling choices

A function-space Bayesian inverse problem should begin with five explicit choices:

  1. Choose the model space \(\M\).
  2. Choose the data space \(\D\).
  3. Choose the forward map \(G\).
  4. Choose the noise model.
  5. Choose the prior measure \(\prior\).

These choices define the mathematical problem. Once the ingredients are chosen, the posterior is defined by Bayes' theorem: \[ \frac{d\post}{d\prior}(m) = \frac{1}{Z(\obs)} \exp(-\Phi(m;\obs)). \] Only after this formula has meaning should we introduce a mesh, basis, matrix, or quadrature rule.

Lesson

The discretization is the numerical approximation of the Bayesian inverse problem. It is not the Bayesian inverse problem itself.

The three common mistakes

The same failure appeared in three disguises:

  1. Treating the prior as just a matrix.
  2. Treating the adjoint as just a transpose.
  3. Treating discretization as formulation.
Mistake 1: Treating the prior as just a matrix

A covariance matrix \(\mathbf{C}_N\in\R^{N\times N}\) may be perfectly valid for every fixed \(N\), but this does not mean that the sequence of priors defines a probability measure on functions. The naive choice \(\mathbf{C}_N=\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_N\) has \(\Tr(\mathbf{C}_N)=\sigma^2 N\to\infty\). In function space, the corresponding identity covariance would satisfy \(\Tr(\sigma^2 I)=\infty\). A function-space Gaussian prior requires a covariance operator that is linear, symmetric, positive, and trace class: \(\Tr(C_0)<\infty\).

Mistake 2: Treating the adjoint as just a transpose

The adjoint is defined by the inner products: \(\inner{G m}{d}_{\D} = \inner{m}{G^{\adj} d}_{\M}\). With a non-orthonormal basis, the discrete adjoint is \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\), not simply \(\Gb^{\T}\).

Mistake 3: Treating discretization as formulation

The dangerous order is: basis \(\Rightarrow\) coefficients \(\Rightarrow\) matrix prior \(\Rightarrow\) posterior. The coherent order is: model space \(\Rightarrow\) prior measure \(\Rightarrow\) posterior measure \(\Rightarrow\) discretization.

The final slogan

Lesson

Think first. Discretize later.

Appendix A: Finite-Dimensional Gaussian Conditioning

This appendix records the finite-dimensional Gaussian conditioning formulas used throughout the document.

Block Gaussian formulas

Let \(\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} \sim \N\left( \begin{pmatrix} \bar{x} \\ \bar{y} \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} C_{xx} & C_{xy} \\ C_{yx} & C_{yy} \end{pmatrix} \right)\), where \(C_{yx}=C_{xy}^{\top}\) and \(C_{yy}\) is invertible. Then the conditional distribution of \(x\) given \(y\) is Gaussian: \(x\mid y \sim \N\left( \bar{x} + C_{xy}C_{yy}^{-1}(y-\bar{y}), \, C_{xx} - C_{xy}C_{yy}^{-1}C_{yx} \right)\).

Intuition

Conditioning a joint Gaussian is a projection of uncertainty. The mean moves toward values compatible with the observed component, and the covariance loses the uncertainty explained by that observation.

Derivation of the posterior mean and covariance

For the finite-dimensional Bayesian linear model \(\mathbf{u}\sim\N(\mathbf{u}_0,\mathbf{C}_N)\) and \(\obs=\Gb\mathbf{u}+\noise\), \(\noise\sim\N(0,\Cd)\), the joint distribution gives:

Posterior mean: \(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_N^{\obs} = \mathbf{u}_0 + \mathbf{C}_N \Gb^{\T} (\Gb \mathbf{C}_N \Gb^{\T} + \Cd)^{-1} (\obs-\Gb\mathbf{u}_0)\).

Posterior covariance: \(\mathbf{C}_N^{\obs} = \mathbf{C}_N - \mathbf{C}_N \Gb^{\T} (\Gb \mathbf{C}_N \Gb^{\T} + \Cd)^{-1} \Gb \mathbf{C}_N\).

The matrix \(\Gb \mathbf{C}_N \Gb^{\T} + \Cd\) is the covariance of the predicted data. The update term is positive semidefinite, so \(0\leq \mathbf{C}_N^{\obs}\leq \mathbf{C}_N\).

Warning

This finite-dimensional formula is correct as linear algebra. The danger begins when \(\mathbf{C}_N\), \(\Gb^{\T}\), and the coefficient space are treated as if they automatically represent a coherent function-space problem.

Appendix B: Gaussian Measures on Hilbert Spaces

Karhunen–Loeve expansions

Let \(m\sim\N(m_0,C_0)\) on a Hilbert space \(\M\), with \(C_0 e_j=\lambda_j e_j\) for an orthonormal basis. The Karhunen-Loeve expansion is \(m = m_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \sqrt{\lambda_j}\,\xi_j e_j\), where \(\xi_j\sim\N(0,1)\) are independent. The expected squared norm is \(\E\norm{m-m_0}_{\M}^2 = \sum \lambda_j = \Tr(C_0)\). The expansion defines a random element of \(\M\) when \(\sum \lambda_j < \infty\).

Intuition

The eigenfunctions are the directions of prior variation. The eigenvalues are the variances assigned to those directions.

Trace-class covariance operators

A positive symmetric operator \(C_0\) is trace class if \(\Tr(C_0) = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \inner{C_0 e_j}{e_j}_{\M} < \infty\). The identity operator is not trace class on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space: \(\Tr(I) = \sum 1 = \infty\). This is why a formal Gaussian prior with covariance \(\sigma^2 I\) does not define a Gaussian probability measure on the Hilbert space itself.

Lesson

Trace class means finite total prior variance. Without it, the Gaussian cloud does not fit inside the chosen Hilbert space.

Cameron–Martin space

The Cameron-Martin space is formally \(\mathcal{H}_{\mu} = \operatorname{Range}(C_0^{1/2})\), equipped with the norm \(\norm{h}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} h_j^2 / \lambda_j\). This space is often smoother or smaller than the space where typical prior samples live.

Warning

The Cameron-Martin space is not usually the same thing as the set of typical samples. It describes admissible shifts of the Gaussian measure, not the rough texture of an ordinary draw.

Appendix C: Discrete Bases and Gram Matrices

Coordinate vectors versus functions

A coefficient vector \(\mathbf{u}\in\R^N\) represents the function \(m_N = \sum_{j=1}^N [\mathbf{u}]_j\phi_j\). The vector \(\mathbf{u}\) is not the function itself. It is a coordinate representation. If the basis changes, the coordinate vector changes, even if the represented function remains the same.

Lesson

Coordinates are names for objects. Changing the names should not change the object.

Mass matrices

The model-space Gram matrix is \([\Mb]_{ij} = \inner{\phi_i}{\phi_j}_{\M}\). For \(\M=L^2(\Omega)\), this is the mass matrix \([\Mb]_{ij} = \int_\Omega \phi_i(x)\phi_j(x)\,dx\). For \(\M=H^1(\Omega)\), it includes derivative terms. The same basis has different Gram matrices in different model-space geometries.

Discrete adjoints

The discrete adjoint is \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}} = \Mb^{-1}\Gb^{\T} \Wb_D\). Only if \(\Mb=\mathbf{I}_N\) and \(\Wb_D=\mathbf{I}_K\) do we recover \(G^{\adj}_{\mathrm{disc}}=\Gb^{\T}\).

Projection and interpolation

Interpolation: \(\mathbf{I}_N m = \sum_{j=1}^N m(x_j)\phi_j\), natural when pointwise values are meaningful. Projection: \(P_N m\in\M_N\) defined by \(\inner{m-P_N m}{v_N}_{\M}=0\) for all \(v_N\in\M_N\), with coefficients satisfying \(M\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\). Projection depends on the model-space inner product; interpolation depends on pointwise evaluation. These are not the same operation.

Warning

Interpolation and projection can produce different discrete functions. Choosing between them is a modelling and numerical decision, not harmless punctuation.

Appendix D: Suggested Exercises

The exercises below are designed to reinforce the main argument. They are not intended as a separate problem set in numerical analysis, but as guided checks on the conceptual machinery.

Exercise set for Act I

  1. Let \(\Omega=[0,1]\) and define \([G m]_i = \int_0^1 K_i(x)\,m(x)\,dx\). Show that if \(K_i\in L^2(0,1)\), then \(G_i\) is a bounded linear functional on \(L^2(0,1)\).
  2. Let \(\{\phi_j\}_{j=1}^N\) be hat functions on a uniform mesh. Write down the matrix entries \(G_{ij}=[G\phi_j]_i\). Explain why this matrix is a representation of \(G\) only after the basis has been chosen.
  3. Suppose \(\mathbf{u}\sim\N(0,\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_N)\). What assumptions about the coefficients are encoded by this prior?
  4. Explain why a plausible posterior mean does not guarantee a plausible posterior measure.
  5. In your own words, distinguish between the unknown function \(m\) and its coefficient vector \(\mathbf{u}\).

Exercise set for Act II

  1. Let \(\mathbf{u}\sim\N(\bar{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{C})\). If \(\mathbf{C}=L\mathbf{L}^{\top}\) and \(\xi\sim\N(0,\mathbf{I})\), show that \(\bar{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{L}\xi\) has mean \(\bar{\mathbf{u}}\) and covariance \(\mathbf{C}\).
  2. Let \(\Gb\in\R^{K\times N}\) with \(N>K\). Show that \(\dim\operatorname{null}(\Gb)\geq N-K\).
  3. For the naive prior \(\mathbf{C}_N=\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_N\), show that if \(\mathbf{v}\in\operatorname{null}(\Gb)\), then \(\mathbf{C}_N^{\obs}\mathbf{v}=\sigma^2\mathbf{v}\).
  4. Prove that \(\Tr(\mathbf{C}_N^{\obs})\geq \sigma^2(N-K)\) under the assumptions of the previous exercise.
  5. Explain why increasing \(N\) with fixed \(K\) creates a problem for the naive posterior.

Exercise set for Act III

  1. List the five modelling choices required before discretization.
  2. Give an example of a model space \(\M\) suitable for functions on \(\Omega=[0,1]\). Explain what kind of models it admits.
  3. Suppose the data are point values \([\obs]_i=m(x_i)+[\noise]_i\). Why might \(L^2(\Omega)\) be an insufficient model space for this forward map?
  4. Define the likelihood potential \(\Phi(m;\obs) = \frac{1}{2} \norm{\obs-G m}_{\Cd^{-1}}^2\). Explain how it enters the posterior density relative to the prior.
  5. Explain the difference between deterministic uniqueness of a model and uniqueness of a posterior measure.

Exercise set for Acts IV–VI

  1. Define a normed space, a Banach space, and a Hilbert space. What extra structure is added at each step?
  2. Let \(\inner{f}{g}_{L^2} = \int_\Omega f(x)g(x)\,dx\). Show that this defines an inner product on \(L^2(\Omega)\), up to equality almost everywhere.
  3. Explain why pointwise values are not generally meaningful for elements of \(L^2(\Omega)\).
  4. Let \(C_0 e_j=\lambda_j e_j\) with \(\{e_j\}\) orthonormal. Show that \(\E\norm{m-m_0}_{\M}^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_j\) for the formal expansion \(m=m_0+\sum \sqrt{\lambda_j}\,\xi_j e_j\).
  5. Explain why \(C_0=\sigma^2 I\) is not trace class on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.
  6. Let \(C_0=\alpha(\kappa^2 I-\Delta)^{-s}\). Explain the roles of \(\alpha\), \(\kappa\), \(s\), and the boundary conditions.
  7. Give an example of a covariance kernel \(k(x,x')\) and explain what qualitative prior information it encodes.

Exercise set for Act VII

  1. Starting from \(\obs=G m+\noise\), write the function-space posterior mean and covariance for a linear Gaussian inverse problem.
  2. Explain why \(G C_0 G^{\adj} + \Cd\) is a data-space covariance.
  3. Suppose \(C_0\) is trace class and \(0\leq C^{\obs}\leq C_0\). Explain why \(C^{\obs}\) is also trace class.
  4. Let \(\M_N\subset\M\) be a finite-dimensional approximation space. Explain what it means for \(\M_N\) to approximate \(\M\) rather than replace it.
  5. Describe how the discrete adjoint changes when the basis functions are not orthonormal.
  6. Compare the naive workflow with the corrected workflow.
  7. In one paragraph, explain the slogan: Think first. Discretize later.
