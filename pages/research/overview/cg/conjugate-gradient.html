The Road to Conjugate Gradients

Gradient descent, diagnosed and repaired — until the conjugate gradient method assembles itself.

1 The Landscape 2 Rolling Downhill 3 The Right Geometry 4 Never Undo Work 5 Conjugate Gradients 6 The Best Descent

What these notes are

The conjugate gradient method is usually met as a recipe: five update formulas, memorized or copied, with a promise that they work astonishingly well. The formulas do work astonishingly well. But presented as a recipe, CG looks like a trick — and tricks are hard to trust, hard to remember, and impossible to adapt.

These notes take the opposite route. We start from the humblest possible question — how do you solve \(Au=f\) when all you can do is apply \(A\)? — and let each difficulty force the next idea. Gradient descent is the obvious first attempt. Watching it fail, precisely and visually, tells us exactly what to fix. By the time the conjugate gradient method appears, there is nothing left to invent: every formula is the unique answer to a question we could not avoid asking.

The promise

By the end, you will not have memorized the conjugate gradient method. You will be unable to imagine it any other way.

Everything is done in a real Hilbert space, not in \(\mathbb R^n\). This is not generality for its own sake: the method genuinely never needs a matrix, a basis, or a mesh — only the action of an operator. Seeing that clearly is half the point, and it connects directly to the Think First, Discretize Later series: the method belongs to the problem, not to its discretization.

The road, in one paragraph

An operator equation is secretly a landscape: solving \(Au=f\) is the same as finding the bottom of a bowl (Act 1). The obvious move is to walk downhill (Act 2) — but downhill zigzags, and the autopsy shows why: it measures “perpendicular” with the wrong ruler (Act 3). The repair is a single design rule — never undo finished work — which forces a new geometry on the search directions (Act 4). Feeding that rule with the only raw material the problem provides, the residuals, collapses into a two-term recurrence (Act 5). What remains is to understand why the result is not merely good but optimal, and what survives in infinite dimensions (Act 6).

What you need to bring

Comfort with inner products and norms, and a willingness to read slowly. Everything else — energy functionals, conjugacy, Krylov subspaces, convergence bounds — is built on the page, from scratch. If you have read any of the other series on this site you will recognize the style: no step is allowed to feel like magic.

Notation and conventions

Remark — Notation

We follow the same typographic doctrine as the other series on this site.

  • The space. \(\H\) is a real Hilbert space with inner product \(\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}\) and norm \(\norm{\cdot}=\sqrt{\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}}\). Elements of \(\H\) (which may be functions) are lightface italic: \(u\), \(v\), \(r_k\), \(p_k\).
  • Operators. Lightface capitals, e.g. \(A:\H\to\H\). We never write a matrix until we explicitly discretize; boldface (\(\mathbf A\), \(\mathbf u\)) is reserved for finite-dimensional objects, and appears only in Act 6.
  • The energy inner product. Once earned (Act 3), \(\ipA{u}{v} := \ip{Au}{v}\) and \(\normA{v}=\sqrt{\ipA{v}{v}}\).
  • Iterates. Subscripts count iterations: \(u_k\) is the \(k\)th iterate, \(r_k = f - Au_k\) its residual, \(p_k\) the \(k\)th search direction.

The six acts

Act 1 — The Landscape Hidden in the Equation. Operator equations are everywhere, and the operator is usually a black box: we can apply it, never invert it. The equation \(Au=f\) is reformulated as the minimization of a quadratic functional \(J\) — a bowl whose unique bottom is the solution, and whose downhill direction is the computable residual.

Act 2 — Rolling Downhill. Gradient descent with exact line search, derived completely: the step length is where a parabola bottoms out. An interactive panel lets you feel the zigzag, and the convergence rate \((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)\) quantifies the pain. The autopsy begins: every turn is a perfect right angle.

Act 3 — The Right Geometry. Why right angles betray us: the landscape measures distance with \(A\), not with the Euclidean ruler. The energy inner product \(\ipA{\cdot}{\cdot}\) is earned, the bowl becomes round in stretched coordinates, and “don’t spoil finished work” acquires an exact mathematical form: \(A\)-orthogonality.

Act 4 — Never Undo Your Work. The method of conjugate directions: line searches along pairwise \(A\)-orthogonal directions make progress permanent — each direction is dealt with once, forever. In \(n\) dimensions the method terminates in \(n\) steps. An interactive panel: pick any first direction; two steps finish the job.

Act 5 — The Conjugate Gradient Method. Where do conjugate directions come from? The residuals are the only new information the operator ever gives us. Orthogonalizing them, almost every Gram–Schmidt term vanishes — a structural miracle that leaves a two-term recurrence. The full algorithm assembles itself, and a race panel shows it demolishing gradient descent.

Act 6 — The Best Possible Descent. CG is optimal — no method using the same information can do better in the energy norm. The polynomial picture explains the famous \(\sqrt{\kappa}\) convergence and why clustered spectra collapse in a few steps. Finally: what survives in infinite dimensions, what discretization does and does not change, and where the honest boundary of the theory lies.

Sources & further reading

The series is self-contained, but nothing in it is new — only the staging is. Each act closes with the references behind its specific claims; the landmarks, all clickable and all worth the detour, are these:

  • Magnus R. Hestenes and Eduard Stiefel (1952). “Methods of Conjugate Gradients for Solving Linear Systems.” Journal of Research of the National Bureau of Standards 49(6), 409–436. The original paper — still remarkably readable. doi:10.6028/jres.049.044 · free PDF (NIST)
  • Jonathan R. Shewchuk (1994). “An Introduction to the Conjugate Gradient Method Without the Agonizing Pain.” Carnegie Mellon University. The kindred spirit of these notes, with many more pictures. free PDF (CMU)
  • Gene H. Golub and Dianne P. O’Leary (1989). “Some History of the Conjugate Gradient and Lanczos Algorithms: 1948–1976.” SIAM Review 31(1), 50–102. Who did what, when, and why — the full story. doi:10.1137/1031003 · author’s reprint PDF
  • Yousef Saad (2003). Iterative Methods for Sparse Linear Systems, 2nd edition. SIAM. The standard modern reference for everything Krylov. doi:10.1137/1.9780898718003 · free PDF (author’s edition)
  • Lloyd N. Trefethen and David Bau III (1997). Numerical Linear Algebra. SIAM. The best-written background for the linear-algebra side. doi:10.1137/1.9780898719574
  • Jorge Nocedal and Stephen J. Wright (2006). Numerical Optimization, 2nd edition. Springer. The optimization side: line searches, steepest descent, CG and beyond. doi:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5
