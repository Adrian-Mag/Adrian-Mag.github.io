My Take on SOLA

Resolving kernels, proxy inversions, and the problem with shifted noise.

What to expect

SOLA is often introduced as a method for estimating localized averages, or more general linear properties, of an unknown model without first reconstructing the whole model. Instead of solving for everything, SOLA tries to build from the data exactly the kind of quantity one wants to report.

The main purpose of these notes is to explain that idea from the ground up, and then to examine carefully what the resulting numbers mean.

The idea has a distinguished lineage, and these notes stand on it. The method of optimally localized averages was created by Backus and Gilbert in a remarkable series of papers [BG67], [BG68], [BG70]. The subtractive variant — SOLA, the subject of these notes — was introduced for helioseismology by Pijpers and Thompson [PT92], [PT94], and brought into seismic tomography by Zaroli and colleagues [Zar16], [ZKL17]. The operator-theoretic framing used throughout, and several of the critical questions these notes pursue, grew out of our own work connecting SOLA to deterministic linear inference [MKZ25]. Full references, with links, close each act.

Lesson

SOLA is best understood as a method for reshuffling linear data information into property-like information. The reshuffled information is real, but it must be labelled honestly.

The central caution

A SOLA calculation begins with target kernels. These describe the linear properties we would like to know. But the method generally returns resolving kernels. These are the linear properties that the available data can actually synthesize.

If the resolving kernel equals the target kernel, then the interpretation is direct. If it does not, then the SOLA number is not the exact target property of the true model. It is the resolving-kernel property of the true model, up to observational noise.

Warning

The target kernel is a design request. The resolving kernel is the answer delivered by the data.

How to read this document

The notes are organized as a guided story. We begin with a simple integral picture, build SOLA in its cleanest form, add normalization constraints and noise, and only later ask the more delicate questions: what did SOLA estimate, what does its uncertainty mean, and what kind of prior information is needed if we want statements about the true target property?

Notation and conventions

Remark — Notation for operators and components

We adopt the following typographic conventions throughout these notes.

  • Finite-dimensional objects. Boldface letters denote finite-dimensional vectors and matrices (coefficient/representation objects). For example \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb R^N\) and \(\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb R^{N\times N}\).
  • Infinite-dimensional objects. Non-bold (lightface) letters denote infinite-dimensional operators or functions acting on the model Hilbert space \(\M\); e.g.\ \(G:\M\to\D\), \(C_0:\M\to\M\).
  • Components / entries. The components of a vector or the entries of a matrix are written with square brackets and subscripts: \[[\mathbf v]_i = v_i,\qquad [\mathbf A]_{ij} = A_{ij}, \qquad i,j=1,\dots,N.\] When an object is represented in a particular basis \(\{\phi_k\}_{k=1}^N\) we always mean the coordinates with respect to that basis; the matrix entries depend on the chosen basis.
  • Inner products. Throughout these notes, all spaces are real Hilbert spaces. We write the inner product on a Hilbert space \(H\) as \((\cdot,\cdot)_H\). Recall that an inner product on a real vector space \(H\) is a map \((\cdot,\cdot)_H:H\times H\to\mathbb R\) that is (i) linear in each argument, (ii) symmetric, \((u,v)_H=(v,u)_H\), and (iii) positive definite, \((v,v)_H\ge0\) with equality iff \(v=0\). The induced norm is \(\|v\|_H=\sqrt{(v,v)_H}\). For the model space \(\M\), data space \(\D\), property space \(\Pspace\), and the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators, we use \((\cdot,\cdot)_{\M}\), \((\cdot,\cdot)_{\D}\), \((\cdot,\cdot)_{\Pspace}\), and \((\cdot,\cdot)_{\HS}\) respectively. The angle-bracket notation \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\) is reserved for dual pairings between a space and its dual, which we do not need here since every Hilbert space is self-dual via the Riesz representation theorem.
  • Adjoints and transposes. We reserve \(^{\top}\) for the Euclidean transpose of a finite matrix and \(^{*}\) for the Hilbert adjoint of an operator. Discrete adjoints that incorporate mass/weight matrices will be written explicitly (for example, the discrete adjoint of \(\mathbf G\) with mass matrix \(\mathbf M\) and data weight \(\mathbf W\) is \(\mathbf G^*=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf G^{\top}\mathbf W\)).
  • Infinite sequences. When working with infinite coefficient sequences we use index notation \((\alpha_i)_{i\ge1}\) or \(\{\alpha_i\}_{i=1}^\infty\); for finite coefficient vectors we write \(\mathbf\alpha=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_N)^\top\) so that \([\mathbf\alpha]_i=\alpha_i\).

The ten acts

Act 1 — The Kernel Game. We begin with an unknown function observed through sensitivity kernels. The data are weighted integrals, and finite data cannot see everything. We introduce target kernels as the questions we wish the data could answer.

Act 2 — Building SOLA from Scratch. Combining data means combining kernels. The resolving kernel is born. We formalize the noiseless unconstrained SOLA problem and derive the normal equation.

Act 3 — Averages Need Mass. A localized kernel must also carry the right total mass. We introduce unimodularity, the average vector, and the constrained SOLA problem with its rank-one correction.

Act 4 — Beyond Averages. Not every target is an average. Derivative targets, basis coefficient targets, and contrasts each need their own resolving constraint.

Act 5 — Noise Enters the Room. Data are not exact. We build the noise-aware SOLA objective, balance resolution against noise amplification, and derive the noisy normal equation.

Act 6 — What Did SOLA Actually Estimate?. The target is the request; the resolving kernel is the answer. We confront the gap between \(\Tau(\bar m)\) and \(\mathcal{A}(\bar m)\), and learn the honest sidestep: interpret through resolving kernels.

Act 7 — A Synthetic Cautionary Tale. A case that looks good: resolving kernels match targets, error bars are small, and yet the SOLA estimates miss the true target properties. The nullspace explains why.

Act 8 — SOLA as an Inversion in Disguise. The constrained SOLA estimate can be written as the target property of a proxy model. We connect resolving kernels to classical resolution operators.

Act 9 — Uncertainty. The propagated covariance \(\mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*\) is not a posterior covariance. Without a prior, there is no Bayesian posterior. We introduce the frequentist interpretation of SOLA as a transformed observation problem with calibrated confidence sets.

Act 10 — What SOLA Is Good For. A fair summary of SOLA's strengths, what it should not be asked to do alone, and the final slogan: \[\text{SOLA transforms the observation problem. It does not solve the inference problem.}\]
