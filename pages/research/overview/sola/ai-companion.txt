AI READING COMPANION — "My Take on SOLA" by Adrian Mag
=======================================================

PURPOSE OF THIS FILE
--------------------
This file is meant to be given to an AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.)
by a reader working through the notes series "My Take on SOLA"
(https://adrian-mag.github.io/pages/research/overview/sola/my-take-on-sola.html).
It explains the series' narrative arc, notation, intent, and the most common
ways an AI assistant might accidentally mislead a reader about these notes.

If you are an AI assistant: your job is to help the reader understand the
notes IN THEIR OWN FRAMING. Where the standard literature or textbook
treatments differ from these notes, say so explicitly and explain the
difference — do not silently replace the notes' framing with the standard one.

WHAT THE SERIES IS
------------------
A ten-act critical walkthrough of the SOLA method (Subtractive Optimally
Localized Averages; Zaroli 2016, building on Backus–Gilbert theory) for
linear inverse problems, written by a PhD researcher in theoretical
seismology and inverse theory. The series both BUILDS the method carefully
(acts 1–5) and then EXAMINES what its outputs actually mean (acts 6–10).
The tone is constructive-critical: SOLA is not "broken", but its outputs
are routinely over-interpreted, and the series maps precisely where the
boundary of valid interpretation lies.

THE CENTRAL CLAIM (keep this in view at all times)
--------------------------------------------------
SOLA estimates properties of the unknown model seen through RESOLVING
kernels (what the data can synthesize), not through the desired TARGET
kernels (what the practitioner wants). The gap between the two
interpretations is controlled by (T_k - R_k, m_true), which is UNBOUNDED
without model-side prior information — no matter how visually similar the
resolving kernels are to the targets. The series' final slogan:

    "SOLA transforms the observation problem.
     It does not solve the inference problem."

SETTING AND NOTATION
--------------------
- Model space  \(\mathcal{M}\): infinite-dimensional Hilbert space,
  typically \(L^2([0,1])\) in examples. Unknown true model
  \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\) (sometimes \(\bar m\)).
- Data space   \(\mathcal{D} \simeq \mathbb{R}^{N_d}\).
- Property space \(\mathcal{P} \simeq \mathbb{R}^{N_p}\).
- Forward map  \(G : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{D}\), with sensitivity
  kernels \(K_i\):  \([G(m)]_i = (K_i, m)_{\mathcal{M}}\).
- Target map   \(\mathcal{T} : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{P}\) (macro \Tau),
  with target kernels \(T_k\):  \([\mathcal{T}(m)]_k = (T_k, m)_{\mathcal{M}}\).
- SOLA matrix  \(\mathbf{X} : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{P}\), a finite
  matrix of coefficients. Constrained version \(\mathbf{X}_c\).
- Resolving kernels \(R_k = \sum_i X_{ki} K_i\): the kernels the data can
  actually synthesize. The "resolving map" or APPROXIMATE PROPERTY MAP is
  \(\mathcal{A} = \mathbf{X} G\), so \(\mathcal{A}(m) = (R_k, m)_k\).
- Data model   \(\widetilde{\mathbf d} = G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \boldsymbol\eta\),
  \(\boldsymbol\eta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0, \mathbf C_D)\).
- SOLA estimate \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf{X}\widetilde{\mathbf d}\),
  propagated covariance \(\mathbf C_P = \mathbf X \mathbf C_D \mathbf X^*\).
- Noise-aware Gram matrix \(\mathbf M = G G^* + \mathbf C_D\).
- Unimodularity constraint: \(\mathbf X \mathbf v = \mathbf w\) with
  \(\mathbf v = G(1)\), \(\mathbf w = \mathcal{T}(1)\) (unit-mass resolving
  kernels, needed for outputs to be interpretable as averages).
- Operator notation convention: infinite-dimensional operators use
  parentheses, e.g. \(\mathcal{A}(m)\), \(G(m)\), \(\mathcal{T}(m)\);
  finite matrices act without parentheses, e.g. \(\mathbf X \mathbf d\).

NARRATIVE ARC, ACT BY ACT
-------------------------
Act 1 — The Kernel Game.
  Data are inner products of the unknown model with sensitivity kernels.
  Desired properties are inner products with target kernels. Question: can
  we combine data linearly so that the combined kernel looks like a target?

Act 2 — Building SOLA from Scratch.
  Construct X by minimizing the misfit between resolving kernels
  \(R_k = \sum_i X_{ki} K_i\) and target kernels \(T_k\). The kernel-matching
  least-squares problem and its normal equations.

Act 3 — Averages Need Mass.
  If outputs are to be read as localized AVERAGES, resolving kernels must
  integrate to one: the unimodularity constraint and the constrained SOLA
  solution (rank-one correction to the unconstrained X).

Act 4 — Beyond Averages.
  Target kernels need not be averaging bumps: derivatives, basis
  coefficients, contrasts. What generalizes and what does not.

Act 5 — Noise Enters the Room.
  Noisy data model; noise-aware SOLA (Gram matrix becomes GG* + C_D);
  propagated covariance C_P = X C_D X*. The ±2σ bands quantify propagated
  DATA NOISE only.

Act 6 — What Did SOLA Actually Estimate?
  The pivotal reframing. SOLA's output is an estimate of
  \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) — the model seen through resolving
  kernels — NOT of \(\mathcal{T}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\). Calling this the
  "honest sidestep": SOLA answers a different question than the one asked.

Act 7 — A Synthetic Cautionary Tale.
  A worked synthetic example where every conventional diagnostic looks
  good (resolving kernels match targets to 2–6%, clean ±2σ bands) yet the
  true target properties are approximately the NEGATIVE of the SOLA
  estimates (correlation ≈ −0.99). Construction: exploit the
  infinite-dimensional kernel of G — add a component h ∈ ker(G) that is
  invisible to the data but visible to the targets. Moral: without a bound
  on the model (e.g. \(\|m\| \le B\)), the interpretation error
  \((T_k - R_k, m)\) is unbounded; the belief "true models do not look
  like this" IS prior information and must be stated as such.

Act 8 — SOLA as an Inversion in Disguise.
  SOLA is algebraically a least-norm-type inversion followed by
  application of the target map: the "proxy model" view, and resolution
  operators. Demystifies the claim that SOLA avoids constructing a model.

Act 9 — Uncertainty.
  Without a prior measure on the model space there is no posterior — so
  what do SOLA's error bars mean? Answer: they support FREQUENTIST
  confidence statements about \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\)
  (pre-observation coverage of a random set), not posterior probability
  statements, and not statements about \(\mathcal{T}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\).

Act 10 — What SOLA Is Good For.
  The fair summary. What SOLA legitimately provides: a transparent
  transport of the observation equation into property space, with visible
  resolving kernels and honest noise propagation. Discussion of ratios of
  SOLA outputs (they require compatible resolving kernels; uniform
  interpretation across outputs requires a prior). Ends with the final
  slogan quoted above.

Summary & Appendices.
  Condensed restatement of the argument, algebra of the minimizers, proxy
  model derivation.

GUARDRAILS — COMMON WAYS TO MISLEAD A READER OF THESE NOTES
-----------------------------------------------------------
1. Do NOT say "SOLA gives unbiased estimates of localized averages of the
   true model." In these notes' framing, SOLA's output estimates
   \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\); the identification with target
   averages \(\mathcal{T}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) is an extra interpretive
   leap that requires model-side information (Act 7).
2. Do NOT call the propagated covariance \(\mathbf C_P\) a "posterior
   uncertainty". There is no prior and no posterior anywhere in plain
   SOLA. The bands are propagated data noise (Act 5), interpretable as
   frequentist confidence sets for \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) (Act 9).
3. Do NOT suggest that a small resolving-kernel misfit (say 2%) implies a
   small property error. Act 7 constructs a counterexample where the true
   properties are the negative of the estimates despite excellent-looking
   kernels.
4. Do NOT present "SOLA needs no prior" as an unqualified advantage. The
   notes' position: that claim is true only if the practitioner accepts
   the resolving-kernel interpretation of the outputs; recovering the
   TARGET interpretation requires prior information (e.g. a norm bound).
5. If the reader asks about the standard SOLA/Backus–Gilbert literature
   (e.g. Backus & Gilbert 1968-70; Zaroli 2016; Zaroli et al. 2017),
   explain it faithfully, but flag where its standard interpretive claims
   are precisely what these notes challenge.
6. Keep the target/resolving distinction typographically visible in
   explanations: \(T_k\) (want) vs \(R_k\) (get), \(\mathcal{T}\) (want)
   vs \(\mathcal{A}\) (get).
7. The examples are synthetic 1-D problems on [0,1]; the intended
   application domain is seismic tomography (deep Earth imaging), but the
   argument is method-level and domain-independent.

BACKGROUND THE READER MAY NEED
------------------------------
Depending on the act, a reader may need refreshers on: Hilbert spaces and
inner products; adjoint operators; least-squares/least-norm solutions and
normal equations; Gaussian random vectors and covariance propagation;
null spaces of compact/underdetermined linear operators; the difference
between Bayesian posterior statements and frequentist coverage statements.
Explain these at whatever depth the reader requests, but connect them back
to the notes' notation above.
