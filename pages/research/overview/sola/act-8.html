The Second Pillar: Resolution Control

The previous act tested the first pillar of SOLA — the claim that no prior information is needed. We showed that the absence of a prior is not a free lunch: it leaves the interpretation gap between resolved properties and true target properties unbounded. The method was not broken, but a boundary was found.

Now we turn to the second pillar: resolution control. SOLA is often presented as offering something that classical linear inversion does not — direct, user-designed control over resolution through the choice of target kernels. Instead of accepting whatever resolution operator a standard inversion happens to produce, the practitioner specifies what kind of localized average or property they want, and SOLA builds the closest resolving kernel the data can support. This is an attractive feature, and it is real. But how different is it, fundamentally, from what classical inversion has been doing all along?

What follows is not an attack on resolution control. It is an examination of what that control actually amounts to — and a reminder that the resolving kernels SOLA produces are not a new kind of object. They are target kernels passed through a resolution operator, viewed from a different angle.

The Proxy Model

From data map to proxy model

One of the standard ways to describe SOLA is to say that it avoids reconstructing the whole model. This is a good description of the practical workflow. SOLA does not ask us to first build a model estimate \(\hat m\) and then evaluate its properties. It goes directly from data to properties: \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d}.\)

But algebra has a habit of opening side doors.

For the constrained SOLA operator, the estimate can also be written in the form \[\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d} = \Tau(\hat m),\] for a particular model \(\hat m\in\M\) — provided that the calibration pairs \((\mathbf v_\ell,\mathbf w_\ell)\) are jointly realizable by some \(f_\ell\in\M\) satisfying \(G(f_\ell)=\mathbf v_\ell\) and \(\Tau(f_\ell)=\mathbf w_\ell\). This is automatic when the constraints are built from test models \(q_\ell\) (take \(f_\ell=q_\ell\)), but for arbitrarily prescribed \((\mathbf V,\mathbf W)\) such \(f_\ell\) need not exist. The proxy model \(\hat m\) is not the true model. It is constructed from the data and from the same ingredients that define the SOLA operator.

So there are two equivalent ways to read the same number: \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) as a direct data-to-property estimate, or \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \Tau(\hat m)\) as the exact target property of a particular proxy model.

Intuition

SOLA walks past the front door of model reconstruction, but the algebra leaves a side entrance open.

This does not make SOLA bad. It makes SOLA more interpretable — and more familiar. The proxy model shows what kind of model-side representative is hiding behind the data-to-property map. As we shall see, that representative is not so different from the one hiding behind classical inversion.

Let \(\mathbf M:=GG^*+\mathbf C_D,\; \mathbf U:=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf V,\; \boldsymbol\Gamma:=\mathbf V^*\mathbf U.\) Recall that the constrained noise-aware SOLA operator is \[\mathbf X_c = \Tau G^*\mathbf M^{-1} - (\Tau G^*\mathbf U-\mathbf W)\boldsymbol\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf U^*.\] Apply this operator to the observed data: \[\mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d} = \Tau G^*\mathbf M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf d} - (\Tau G^*\mathbf U-\mathbf W)\boldsymbol\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf U^*\widetilde{\mathbf d}.\] Define the coefficient vector \(\mathbf c := \boldsymbol\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf U^*\widetilde{\mathbf d}\in\mathbb R^L.\) Then \[\mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d} = \Tau G^*\mathbf M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf d} - (\Tau G^*\mathbf U-\mathbf W)\mathbf c.\]

If we can find models \(f_1,\dots,f_L\in\M\) satisfying \(G(f_\ell)=\mathbf v_\ell,\; \Tau(f_\ell)=\mathbf w_\ell,\; \ell=1,\dots,L,\) then \(\mathbf W\mathbf c = \sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\,\mathbf w_\ell = \sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\,\Tau(f_\ell) = \Tau\!\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell f_\ell\right),\) and similarly \(\Tau G^*\mathbf U\,\mathbf c = \Tau\!\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\, G^*\mathbf u_\ell\right),\) where \(\mathbf u_\ell\) are the columns of \(\mathbf U\). Therefore \[\mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d} = \Tau\!\left( G^*\mathbf M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf d} + \sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\bigl(f_\ell - G^*\mathbf u_\ell\bigr) \right).\] This motivates the definition \[\hat m := G^*\mathbf M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf d} + \sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\bigl(f_\ell - G^*\mathbf u_\ell\bigr), \qquad \mathbf c=\boldsymbol\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf U^*\widetilde{\mathbf d}.\] Then \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d} = \Tau(\hat m).\)

Checkpoint

The constrained SOLA estimate can be interpreted as the exact target property of a proxy model \(\hat m\), provided the calibration pairs are jointly realizable in \(\M\).

This statement should be handled carefully. It does not say \(\hat m=\bar m.\) It only says that the SOLA property estimate can be factored through one particular model-space representative.

The proxy formula

Let us inspect the proxy model: \[\hat m = G^*\mathbf M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf d} + \sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\bigl(f_\ell - G^*\mathbf u_\ell\bigr).\]

It has two pieces.

The first piece is \(G^*\mathbf M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf d}.\) This resembles a regularized or noise-weighted back-projection of the data into the model space. It is the model-space object produced by taking the data, weighting them by \(\mathbf M^{-1}\), and applying the adjoint \(G^*\).

The second piece is \(\sum_{\ell=1}^L c_\ell\bigl(f_\ell - G^*\mathbf u_\ell\bigr).\) This is the correction associated with the resolving constraints \(\mathbf X_c\mathbf V=\mathbf W.\) The coefficient vector \(\mathbf c=\boldsymbol\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf U^*\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) depends on the data, while the model-space directions \(f_\ell - G^*\mathbf u_\ell\) depend on the constraints.

The functions \(f_\ell\in\M\) are chosen so that \(G(f_\ell)=\mathbf v_\ell,\; \Tau(f_\ell)=\mathbf w_\ell.\) For averaging constraints, this says that each \(f_\ell\) has the desired data response to the corresponding test model and the desired property response. In the simplest average-preserving case, the \(f_\ell\) play the role of model-space representatives that make the constraints visible inside \(\Tau\).

Remark — Non-uniqueness of \(f_\ell\)

The functions \(f_\ell\) need not be unique. If \(f_\ell\) and \(f_\ell'\) both satisfy the conditions for a given \(\ell\), their difference lies in the joint nullspace \(f_\ell-f_\ell'\in\kernel(G)\cap\kernel(\Tau).\) Changing \(f_\ell\) by such a direction changes the proxy model, but not its data response or target property. This is another reminder that the proxy model is not a unique physical truth.

In the noise-free case, where \(\mathbf C_D=\mathbf 0,\;\text{and hence}\; \mathbf M=GG^*,\) we have \(G(G^*\mathbf u_\ell) = GG^*(GG^*)^{-1}\mathbf v_\ell = \mathbf v_\ell.\) Since \(G(f_\ell)=\mathbf v_\ell\), each correction direction satisfies \(G\bigl(G^*\mathbf u_\ell-f_\ell\bigr)=\mathbf 0.\) Thus, in the noise-free constrained case, each correction lives in the nullspace of \(G\).

In the noise-aware case, the situation is slightly different. Since \(\mathbf M=GG^*+\mathbf C_D,\) we have \(GG^*\mathbf u_\ell = \mathbf v_\ell-\mathbf C_D\mathbf u_\ell.\) Therefore \(G\bigl(G^*\mathbf u_\ell-f_\ell\bigr) = -\mathbf C_D\mathbf u_\ell.\) So the corrections are not generally exact nullspace corrections when data noise is included. Rather, they are noise-weighted corrections tied to the SOLA objective.

Warning

In the noise-free constrained case, the proxy correction is a true nullspace correction. In the noise-aware case, it is better interpreted as a correction relative to the noise-weighted SOLA geometry.

This distinction matters because it prevents us from saying something too strong. The proxy model is a useful algebraic representative, not a magical recovery of the true model.

The meaning of the proxy

The proxy model \(\hat m\) is not claimed to be the true model. It is one model-space object whose target property equals the SOLA estimate: \(\Tau(\hat m)=\widetilde{\mathbf p}.\)

This gives a second interpretation of SOLA: \[\text{direct view:} \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X_c\widetilde{\mathbf d},\] and \[\text{proxy-model view:} \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf p} = \Tau(\hat m).\]

The direct view is the standard SOLA story. It emphasizes that the method avoids a full model reconstruction. The proxy-model view emphasizes that the same data-to-property map can be factored through a particular model-space representative.

Both views are useful. The first explains why SOLA is computationally attractive. The second explains why SOLA is not entirely separate from classical inversion.

If the true model \(\bar m\) is close to the proxy model \(\hat m\), in the geometry relevant to \(\Tau\), then \(\Tau(\bar m)\approx \Tau(\hat m)=\widetilde{\mathbf p}.\) But if \(\bar m\) differs from \(\hat m\) by a direction that is hard for the data to see and easy for \(\Tau\) to see, then \(\Tau(\bar m)\) can be very different from \(\Tau(\hat m).\)

Thus the proxy interpretation does not remove the caution from the previous act. It sharpens it.

Lesson

SOLA may avoid explicitly reconstructing the model, but its algebra often carries an undercover proxy reconstruction.

The proxy model is not the villain. It is a useful witness. It tells us what kind of model the SOLA estimate is silently willing to be the property of.

Resolving Kernels and Resolution Operators

Classical resolution operators

In a classical linear inversion, one builds a reconstruction operator \(G^\dagger:\D\to\M.\) Given data \(\mathbf d\in\D\), the reconstructed model is \(\hat m=G^\dagger\mathbf d.\) If the data are noise-free and generated by a true model \(m\), then \(\mathbf d=G(m),\) so the reconstruction is \(\hat m=G^\dagger G(m).\) The composite map \(\mathcal R:=G^\dagger G:\M\to\M\) is called the resolution operator.

The resolution operator tells us what the inversion does to the true model: \(m \longmapsto \mathcal R m.\) If \(\mathcal R=I\), then the inversion recovers every model exactly. In finite-data inverse problems, this almost never happens. More commonly, \(\mathcal R\) is a smoothing, filtering, or projection-like operator.

Intuition

A resolution operator is the fingerprint left by the inverse method on the model. It tells us not what model we wished to recover, but what version of the model the data and method can return.

This is the classical resolution story: \[\text{true model} \quad \xrightarrow{\;\mathcal R\;} \quad \text{resolved model}.\] The reconstructed model \(\hat m\) should therefore be interpreted as \(\hat m=\mathcal R m\) in the ideal noise-free case, not automatically as \(m\).

This is old knowledge. Classical inversion has carried this scar for decades: the method does not return the true model, it returns a resolved version of it. Everyone who uses least squares or regularized inversion knows this. It is part of the working knowledge of the field.

SOLA resolving kernels

SOLA defines the approximate property map \(\mathcal{A}:=\mathbf XG:\M\to\Pspace.\) For each component, \([\mathcal{A}(m)]_k = (R_k,m)_{\M},\) where \(R_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N_d}[\mathbf X]_{ki}K_i.\) The functions \(R_k\) are the resolving kernels.

Thus SOLA has the property-space resolution story \[\text{true model} \quad \xrightarrow{\;\mathcal{A}=\mathbf XG\;} \quad \text{resolved properties}.\]

The target map is \(\Tau:\M\to\Pspace.\) If SOLA achieved perfect resolution, then \(\mathcal{A}=\Tau.\) But in general, \(\mathcal{A}\neq\Tau.\) The resolving kernels describe the actual linear questions asked of the model.

Here is where the second pillar's claim gets interesting. SOLA is said to offer resolution control: the practitioner chooses target kernels, and the method builds the closest resolving kernels the data can support. This is contrasted with classical inversion, where the resolution operator is whatever the inversion produces — take it or leave it. But is the contrast as sharp as it sounds?

Now connect this with the proxy-model view. Suppose there exists a linear reconstruction-like operator \(G^\dagger:\D\to\M\) such that \(\mathbf X=\Tau G^\dagger.\) Then \(\mathcal{A}=\mathbf XG=\Tau G^\dagger G.\) Using the classical resolution operator \(\mathcal R:=G^\dagger G,\) we obtain \(\mathcal{A}=\Tau\mathcal R.\)

This identity is the bridge: \[\boxed{\text{SOLA resolving map} = \text{target map after a model-space resolution operator}.}\]

At the kernel level, if \([\Tau(m)]_k = (T_k,m)_{\M},\) then \([\mathcal{A}(m)]_k = [\Tau(\mathcal R m)]_k = (T_k,\mathcal R m)_{\M}.\) Using the adjoint \(\mathcal R^*\), \([\mathcal{A}(m)]_k = (\mathcal R^*T_k,m)_{\M}.\) Therefore the resolving kernel is \(R_k = \mathcal R^*T_k.\)

This is a revealing formula, when it applies. The SOLA resolving kernel is the target kernel passed through the adjoint of a resolution operator. Not a different kind of resolution. Not a new species of object. The same resolution operator that classical inversion has always carried — just viewed from the other side. The caveat is that this factorization \(\mathbf X=\Tau G^\dagger\) need not hold for every SOLA map; when it does, the bridge formula \(R_k=\mathcal R^*T_k\) is available, and when it does not, the resolving kernels are still well-defined but cannot be written in this compact form.

Checkpoint

Classical inversion modifies models through \(\mathcal R\). SOLA modifies target kernels through \(\mathcal R^*\). Same operator, different adjoint direction.

The shared resolution story

So what does the second pillar actually claim, and what does the algebra say?

The claim is that SOLA offers better resolution control than classical inversion. Instead of accepting whatever resolution operator the inversion produces, the practitioner designs target kernels and SOLA finds the closest resolving kernels. The resolving kernels are put on the table, inspected, and compared to the targets. This is a genuine practical advantage.

But the algebra says: whenever the SOLA map factors as \(\mathbf X=\Tau G^\dagger\), the resolving kernels are not a new kind of object. They are target kernels mapped through the adjoint of a resolution operator. Classical inversion modifies the model through \(\mathcal R\); SOLA modifies the target kernel through \(\mathcal R^*\). The underlying operator is the same. The data geometry, the sensitivity kernels, the noise covariance — these still decide what is reachable. SOLA does not escape the resolution operator. It views it from the adjoint side.

We can now compare the two pictures directly.

Classical inversion says: \(\mathbf d=G(m),\; \hat m=G^\dagger\mathbf d,\; \hat m=G^\dagger G(m)=\mathcal R m.\) The method does not return \(m\). It returns \(\mathcal R m\).

SOLA says: \(\mathbf d=G(m),\; \widetilde{\mathbf p}=\mathbf X\mathbf d,\; \widetilde{\mathbf p}=\mathbf XG(m)=\mathcal{A}(m).\) The method does not necessarily return \(\Tau(m)\). It returns \(\mathcal{A}(m)\).

If the SOLA map factors as \(\mathbf X=\Tau G^\dagger,\) then \(\mathcal{A}=\Tau\mathcal R.\) In that case, the SOLA estimate is the target property of the resolved model: \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \Tau(\mathcal R m).\) Equivalently, it is the true model tested against resolved target kernels: \([\widetilde{\mathbf p}]_k = (\mathcal R^*T_k,m)_{\M}.\)

These are two sides of the same coin: \(\Tau(\mathcal R m) \quad\text{and}\quad (\Tau\mathcal R)(m).\)

Intuition

Classical inversion blurs the model and then asks the target question. SOLA can be read as blurring the target question and then asking it of the true model. Same blur, different order of operations.

What is genuinely different

There is a real advantage here, and it should not be understated. SOLA lets us design the target question and inspect the blurred version that the data can actually support. Instead of hiding resolution inside a reconstructed model, SOLA puts the resolving kernels on the table. That is useful. It is a genuine ergonomic and interpretational improvement over classical inversion, where the resolution operator may be implicit or harder to inspect.

But the limitation is that this is still resolution-limited information. The data do not grant perfect access to \(\Tau(m)\) just because we avoided reconstructing \(m\). They grant access to \(\mathcal{A}(m)=\mathbf XG(m),\) which is a resolved version of the desired property. The resolving kernel is the target kernel after \(\mathcal R^*\). The resolution operator has not been bypassed — it has been moved to a different place in the formula.

Warning

Avoiding an explicit model reconstruction does not avoid resolution. It only changes where the resolution appears.

This is why the slogan that SOLA gives “resolution control” should be read carefully. SOLA does give meaningful control over the resolving kernels — the practitioner chooses targets and inspects what the data can deliver. That is a real advantage over methods that bury resolution inside an opaque reconstructed model. But it is not a new kind of resolution. The resolving kernels are target kernels passed through \(\mathcal R^*\). The sensitivity kernels, noise covariance, constraints, and data geometry still decide what is reachable. SOLA does not give sovereignty over resolution. It gives visibility.

Lesson

SOLA does not bypass the resolution operator. It views it from the adjoint side. The resolving kernel is the target kernel after \(\mathcal R^*\). That is useful, but it is not a different species of resolution.

The next act turns from resolution to uncertainty — the third pillar. Once again, the theme will be the same: the formula is mathematically correct, but its interpretation needs careful labels.
