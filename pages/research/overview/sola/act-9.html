The Third Pillar: Uncertainty Quantification

The first act of this series tested the “no prior” pillar and found a boundary: without model-side information, the interpretation gap is unbounded. The second act tested the “resolution control” pillar and found that SOLA's resolving kernels are target kernels passed through the adjoint of a resolution operator — useful, but not a new species of resolution.

Now we turn to the third pillar: uncertainty quantification. SOLA is said to come with built-in uncertainty estimates. The propagated covariance \(\mathbf C_P=\mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*\) is presented as a quantification of uncertainty in the SOLA output, often displayed as error bars on the estimated properties. This is an attractive feature, and the calculation itself is correct. But what question does it actually answer? And is that the question we want to ask?

The distinction we need here is a basic one, but it is easy to slip past. Covariances do not ask or answer questions. Measures do. A random variable or a probability measure is tied to a specific question — “what is the distribution of the data noise?” or “what do we believe about the true model given the data?” — and the associated measure provides the answer. A covariance is a summary statistic of that measure, not a substitute for it. Two different questions come with two different measures, and their covariances are not interchangeable even if the matrices happen to look similar.

What follows is not an attack on uncertainty quantification. It is an examination of what question the propagated covariance actually answers, and a reminder that this is not the same question as “what do we know about the true target property?”

What SOLA Actually Does for Uncertainty

The shifted-noise pushforward

Let us start with a simple observation that is easy to miss. In the noisy setting, the observed data are \(\widetilde{\mathbf d} = G(\bar m)+\boldsymbol\eta,\; \boldsymbol\eta\sim N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf C_D),\) where \(\bar m\in\M\) is the unknown true model and \(\mathbf C_D\) is the data-noise covariance. The SOLA estimate is \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d}.\) The standard uncertainty display is the covariance \[\mathbf C_P = \mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*,\] shown as error bars \([\widetilde{\mathbf p}]_k \pm 2\sqrt{[\mathbf C_P]_{kk}}.\) This is the advertised uncertainty quantification — the third pillar.

Now here is the observation. Once the data \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) have been observed, one can construct a probability measure on data space by centring the noise law at the observation: \[\mathbf z_{\mathrm{shift}} = \widetilde{\mathbf d}+\boldsymbol\xi,\; \boldsymbol\xi\sim N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf C_D).\] This is the shifted-noise measure \(N(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D)\) — the noise distribution shifted so that its mean is the observed data. Pushing it through \(\mathbf X\) gives \[N(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D) \xrightarrow{\;\mathbf X\;} N(\mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*).\]

That is exactly the SOLA uncertainty display. The measure \(N(\mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_P)\) is the pushforward of the shifted-noise measure. The covariance \(\mathbf C_P\) is a summary statistic of this pushforward measure.

Checkpoint

By default, SOLA's uncertainty quantification is the pushforward of the shifted-noise measure \(N(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D)\) through the SOLA matrix \(\mathbf X\). The covariance \(\mathbf C_P\) is a summary statistic of that pushforward.

This is mathematically valid. The pushforward exists, the covariance is correctly computed, and the error bars are a faithful summary of this particular measure. Nothing is wrong with the calculation.

But now the real question arrives. What is the point of this measure? What question does it answer? And is that a question worth asking in the context of an inverse problem?

What is the shifted-noise measure?

The shifted-noise construction starts from the realized observation \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) and spreads probability mass around it: \(\widetilde{\mathbf d} \quad\leadsto\quad N(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D).\) This is an observation-centred object. It lives on data space, not model space. It says: “take the data we happened to observe, and wiggle them as if we could draw another set of noise from the same law.”

The question it answers is: \[\text{what happens if I jitter the observed data by another draw from the noise law?}\]

That is a legitimate computational question. But it is not the question behind the inverse problem. The inverse problem does not ask what happens if we re-observe. It asks what the observation tells us about the model — or about a property of the model.

Intuition

The shifted-noise measure says, “wiggle the observation.” The inverse problem asks, “what models and properties are compatible with this observation?” These are different questions, and they come with different measures.

The generative question

The noise distribution should be seen only as auxiliary - it helps us answer the question "If I had the perfect noiseless measurement, what would I expect to actually measure in the noisy imperfect world?". Together with the forward mapping, it allows us to answer a deeper question: \[\text{if }m\text{ were the true model, what data should I expect to observe?}\]

In the additive Gaussian case, the answer is \(\mathbf d_{\mathrm{obs}} \mid m \sim N(G(m),\mathbf C_D).\) The distribution of possible observations is centred at \(G(m)\), not at the realized \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\). This is the generative direction: model first, then data.

In a probabilistic setting, this model-data relation is a likelihood kernel \(L(m,\cdot),\) where, for each \(m\in\M\), \(L(m,\cdot)\) is a probability measure on the data space \(\D\). In the additive Gaussian case, \(L(m,\cdot) = N(G(m),\mathbf C_D).\)

In a set-valued setting, the analogous object is a set likelihood \(\mathrm{L}(m)\subseteq\D.\) For additive bounded noise, \(\mathrm{L}(m) = G(m)+S_{\mathrm{noise}},\) where \(S_{\mathrm{noise}}\subseteq\D\) is the set of allowed noise values. Both versions share the same architecture: \(m \quad\longmapsto\quad \text{possible data under }m.\)

Intuition

The generative measure starts from a possible model and describes what that model could produce in data space. The shifted-noise measure starts from the observation and wiggles it. One is model-to-data. The other is data-to-data. Only one of them is the right foundation for inference.

Why the shifted-noise measure is not useful

One might object: “But the generative perspective also produces a measure on data space. If we restrict attention to models whose noiseless data \(G(m)\) is close to the observed \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\), then the likelihood measure \(L(m,\cdot)\) for those models is approximately \(N(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D)\) — which is the shifted-noise measure. And those models should be the most likely ones, right?”

This is a natural thought, but it does not survive scrutiny. We already agreed that the noise is modeled probabilistically. The observed data \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) is a realization of a random variable whose distribution depends on \( m\) and is governed by the likelihood \(L( m,\cdot)\). There is no reason to assume that the observed data is close to the true noiseless data \(G( m)\). It might be. It might not be. That is what “probabilistic noise” means — the observation is not necessarily the closest thing to the truth. It is one draw from a distribution.

So the observed data could, in principle, be used to update a prior on \(\M\) — that is what Bayesian inference does. But SOLA has no prior. That was the first pillar we examined, and the boundary we found there now returns: without a prior (or some other model-side information), there is no mechanism to turn the likelihood into a posterior. The observation cannot, by itself, tell us which models are more likely. It can only tell us which models are compatible — and compatibility is not probability.

Paradoxically, then, the observed data itself does not seem to be of much use in the SOLA framework. Without a prior, there is nothing to update. The shifted-noise measure is a data-space object with no model-space counterpart to connect it to. It is not a posterior. It is not a likelihood. It is a jitter around an observation that cannot be turned into a statement about the model.

Warning

The shifted-noise pushforward is not a posterior measure. It cannot become one without a prior. And SOLA, by design, does not have one. The observation cannot update what is not there.

This is the boundary of the third pillar. The propagated covariance is the covariance of a shifted-noise pushforward. The calculation is correct. The measure is well-defined. But without a prior, it cannot be turned into a statement about the model or its properties. It answers “what if we re-observed?” — and that is not the question the inverse problem asks.

Lesson

Without a prior, the observed data cannot be turned into a posterior. The shifted-noise measure is a data-space object with no model-space bridge. Its covariance is a summary of a measure that answers the wrong question.

So where does that leave us? If the observed data cannot be used to build uncertainty on the model side, and if the shifted-noise pushforward is not useful, then we should stop trying to extract meaning from the observation and return to the core idea of SOLA: reshuffling information. But this time, instead of reshuffling the observation at the level of kernels, we should apply that idea to the likelihood itself. The next act develops this generative reinterpretation — where SOLA transforms the model-data relation into a model-property relation, and uncertainty is carried by the right measure from the start.
