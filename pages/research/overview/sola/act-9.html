No prior, no posterior — but a frequentist confidence story that works.

The Third Pillar: Uncertainty Quantification

The first act of this series tested the “no prior” pillar and found a boundary: without model-side information, the interpretation gap is unbounded. The second act tested the “resolution control” pillar and found that SOLA's resolving kernels are target kernels passed through the adjoint of a resolution operator — useful, but not a new species of resolution.

Now we turn to the third pillar: uncertainty quantification. SOLA is said to come with built-in uncertainty estimates. The propagated covariance \(\mathbf C_P=\mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*\) is presented as a quantification of uncertainty in the SOLA output, often displayed as error bars on the estimated properties. This is an attractive feature, and the calculation itself is correct. But what question does it actually answer? And is that the question we want to ask?

What SOLA Does in Practice for Uncertainty

The shifted-noise pushforward

Let us start with a simple observation that is easy to miss. In the noisy setting, the observed data are \(\widetilde{\mathbf d} = G(\bar m)+\boldsymbol\eta,\; \boldsymbol\eta\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0,\mathbf C_D),\) where \(\bar m\in\M\) is the unknown true model and \(\mathbf C_D\) is the data-noise covariance. The SOLA estimate is \(\widetilde{\mathbf p} = \mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d}.\) The standard uncertainty display is the covariance \[\mathbf C_P = \mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*,\] shown as error bars \([\widetilde{\mathbf p}]_k \pm 2\sqrt{[\mathbf C_P]_{kk}}.\) This is the advertised uncertainty quantification — the third pillar. (The display itself is standard across the SOLA literature, from the original error propagation [PT92] to tomographic practice [Zar16].)

Now here is the observation. Once the data \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) have been observed, one can construct a probability measure on data space by centring the noise law at the observation: \[\mathbf z_{\mathrm{shift}} = \widetilde{\mathbf d}+\boldsymbol\xi,\; \boldsymbol\xi\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0,\mathbf C_D).\] This is the shifted-noise measure \(\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D)\) — the noise distribution shifted so that its mean is the observed data. Pushing it through \(\mathbf X\) gives \[\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D) \xrightarrow{\;\mathbf X\;} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf X\mathbf C_D\mathbf X^*).\]

That is exactly the SOLA uncertainty display. The measure \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_P)\) is the pushforward of the shifted-noise measure. The covariance \(\mathbf C_P\) is a summary statistic of this pushforward measure.

Checkpoint

By default, SOLA's uncertainty quantification is the pushforward of the shifted-noise measure \(\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D)\) through the SOLA matrix \(\mathbf X\). The covariance \(\mathbf C_P\) is a summary statistic of that pushforward.

This is mathematically valid. The pushforward exists, the covariance is correctly computed, and the error bars are a faithful summary of this particular measure. Nothing is wrong with the calculation.

But now the real question arrives. What is the point of this measure? What question does it answer? And is that a question worth asking in the context of an inverse problem?

What does the pushforward measure mean?

We know by now that SOLA targets the approximate property \(\mathcal{A}(m)\), not the desired property \(\Tau(m)\). That distinction was established in earlier acts. The question now is narrower: what does the pushforward measure \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf X\widetilde{\mathbf d}, \mathbf C_P)\) say probabilistically about the approximate property \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\)? Should we interpret it as “the approximate property of the true model is distributed according to this measure”? It is not a posterior measure on the model or its properties — that much is clear, and we will see why shortly. But the plotted interval \([\widetilde{\mathbf p}]_k \pm 2\sqrt{[\mathbf C_P]_{kk}}\) does have a perfectly legitimate frequentist interpretation as a confidence interval for the resolved quantity \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\). We will derive that interpretation later in this act. The point of the present discussion is not to say the interval is meaningless, but to clarify what it is and is not: it is not a Bayesian posterior, but it can be a calibrated frequentist confidence statement.

No Prior, No Posterior

The shifted-noise measure \(\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\mathbf d},\mathbf C_D)\) is a probability measure on data space. Pushing it through \(\mathbf X\) gives a probability measure on property space. But what does that measure mean?

If we had a prior on the model space \(\M\), we could combine it with the likelihood to form a posterior. The observed data \(\widetilde{\mathbf d}\) would update the prior into a posterior measure on \(m\), and pushing that posterior through \(\mathcal{A}\) would give a posterior on the approximate property \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\). That is the Bayesian route, and it is the only route that produces a genuine posterior probability measure on the model or its properties. (For the Bayesian formulation of inverse problems — and the central, non-negotiable role of the prior in it — the standard reference is Tarantola [Tar05].)

But SOLA, by design, does not introduce a prior. Without a prior, the observation cannot be turned into a posterior statement about the approximate property \(\mathcal{A}(m_{\mathrm{true}})\).

Warning

The shifted-noise pushforward is not a posterior measure. It cannot become one without a prior. And SOLA, by design, does not have one. The observation cannot update what is not there.

So where does that leave us? If the Bayesian route is closed — because SOLA does not use a prior — then we need a different framework for uncertainty. We need a framework that does not require a prior, that works with a fixed true model and random observations, and that produces calibrated statements about the quantities SOLA actually targets.

That framework is frequentist inference.

Intuition

The Bayesian asks: “given what I observed, what do I believe about the model?” The frequentist asks: “if the true model is fixed, how often would my procedure produce a correct statement?” SOLA, without a prior, is naturally suited to the second question.

If you are not familiar with the frequentist framework — or if you want to see how it differs from the Bayesian approach in more detail — I have written a separate series of pages on Bayesian and Frequentist Inference. The key idea is simple: the true model is fixed but unknown, the observation is random because of noise, and uncertainty is quantified by the long-run frequency with which a procedure produces correct statements. What follows applies that framework to SOLA.

SOLA as a Transformed Observation Problem

In the frequentist interpretation, the true model \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\in\M\) is fixed but unknown. It is not assigned a prior measure and is not treated as a random variable. The randomness comes from the observation process.

The original data equation is \[\Dobsrand = G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \Nrand,\] where \(\Nrand\sim\muN.\) After observation, the realized equation is \[\dobs = G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \nobs.\]

SOLA applies a linear transformation \(\mathbf X:\D\to\Pspace\) to the data. Before observation, this gives \[\Prand = \mathbf X\Dobsrand = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \mathbf X\Nrand.\] After observation, this gives \[\pobs = \mathbf X\dobs = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \mathbf X\nobs.\]

Define the transformed noise random variable \(\etarand = \mathbf X\Nrand\) with law \(\mueta = \mathbf X_{\#}\muN.\) The realized transformed noise is \(\etaobs = \mathbf X\nobs.\)

Thus the transformed observation equation is \[\Prand = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \etarand.\] The model \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\) is fixed. The transformed signal \(\mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) is fixed. The transformed observation \(\Prand\) is random because the transformed noise \(\etarand\) is random.

If the experiment were repeated many times with the same fixed model \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\), one would obtain \[\Prand_j = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \etarand_j, \qquad j=1,2,3,\dots.\] Each repetition would produce a different transformed observation because each repetition would have a different noise realization.

Checkpoint

This is the frequentist setting for SOLA. The true model is fixed. The SOLA-induced quantity \(\mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) is fixed. The transformed observation \(\Prand = \mathbf X\Dobsrand\) is random. The realized transformed datum \(\pobs = \mathbf X\dobs\) is what we actually observe.

Confidence Sets in Property Space

Suppose first that we are interested directly in the SOLA-induced approximate property \(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}).\) Then the transformed observation equation is \[\Prand = \AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \etarand.\]

This has the same form as the original additive-noise problem, but now in property space. If \(F_{\alpha}\subseteq\Pspace\) is a transformed-noise acceptance region satisfying \(\mueta(F_{\alpha}) = 1 - \alpha,\) then a confidence set for the approximate property is \[C_{\alpha}^{\mathbf X}(\pobs) = \left\{\mathbf p\in\Pspace : \pobs - \mathbf p \in F_{\alpha}\right\}.\] Equivalently, \(C_{\alpha}^{\mathbf X}(\pobs) = \pobs - F_{\alpha}.\)

This set contains the transformed signal values \(\mathbf p\) for which the observation \(\pobs\) can be explained by an accepted transformed-noise realization.

Coverage

Before observation, define the random transformed confidence set \[C_{\alpha}^{\mathbf X}(\Prand) = \left\{\mathbf p\in\Pspace : \Prand - \mathbf p \in F_{\alpha}\right\}.\]

Coverage in this pre-observation sense is exactly Neyman’s original construction of confidence sets [Ney37] — the construction these notes examine at length in the frequentist series.

By definition, \(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) \in C_{\alpha}^{\mathbf X}(\Prand)\) if and only if \(\Prand - \AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) \in F_{\alpha}.\) But \(\Prand = \AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \etarand,\) so \(\Prand - \AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) = \etarand.\) Therefore \[\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) \in C_{\alpha}^{\mathbf X}(\Prand) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \etarand \in F_{\alpha}.\]

Taking probabilities gives \[\Prob_{m_{\mathrm{true}}}\!\left(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) \in C_{\alpha}^{\mathbf X}(\Prand)\right) = \Prob\!\left(\etarand \in F_{\alpha}\right) = \mueta(F_{\alpha}) = 1 - \alpha.\]

Frequentist SOLA coverage

If the true model \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\) is fixed and the noisy observation process is repeated many times, then the transformed confidence set catches the fixed transformed signal \(\mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) with frequency \(1 - \alpha\).

This is the direct frequentist SOLA coverage statement for the approximate property \(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}).\)

What SOLA Does and Does Not Provide

Without a prior, SOLA can provide:

  • Transformed observation: the actual quantity \(\pobs = \mathbf X\dobs.\)
  • Transformed noise law: the pushforward measure \(\mueta = \mathbf X_{\#}\muN.\)
  • Approximate property: the noiseless transformed signal \(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) = \mathbf X G(m_{\mathrm{true}}).\)
  • Frequentist confidence statements: calibrated coverage statements for confidence sets targeting \(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}).\)

What SOLA does not automatically provide:

  • No posterior on models. There is no posterior measure on \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\) unless a prior has been introduced.
  • No posterior on properties. There is no posterior measure on \(\AX(m_{\mathrm{true}})\) unless a posterior on models has been constructed and pushed forward.
  • No immunity to outliers. The transformed observation \(\pobs\) may still be affected by an unlucky realization of transformed noise.
Lesson

SOLA transforms the observation problem from data space to property space: \(\dobs = G(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \nobs \;\longmapsto\; \pobs = \AX(m_{\mathrm{true}}) + \etaobs.\) After that transformation, the same conceptual choice remains: take the Bayesian route (put a prior on \(m\), condition on \(\dobs\), push the posterior through \(\AX\)), or take the frequentist route (keep \(m_{\mathrm{true}}\) fixed, push the noise through \(\mathbf X\), and build calibrated confidence procedures for the transformed quantities).

This is why SOLA is naturally frequentist in this interpretation. It does not produce a posterior distribution over models or properties. It transforms the observation equation and the noise law, then uses the transformed problem to build calibrated confidence and decision procedures. The shifted-noise pushforward — the object SOLA reports by default — is not wrong, but it is not a posterior. It is a frequentist object that happens to look like one. The frequentist interpretation makes its meaning precise.
